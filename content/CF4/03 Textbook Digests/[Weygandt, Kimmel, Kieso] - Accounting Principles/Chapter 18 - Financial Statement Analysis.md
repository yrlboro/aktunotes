# Weygandt — Chapter 18: Financial Statement Analysis

> [!ABSTRACT] Chapter in One View
> Chapter 18 mengubah financial statements dari sekadar kumpulan angka menjadi **decision tools**. Chapter dimulai dari ide bahwa angka perusahaan baru berarti jika dibandingkan: dengan periode sebelumnya, dengan perusahaan lain, atau dengan benchmark yang relevan. Weygandt lalu memperkenalkan tiga tools utama—**horizontal analysis, vertical analysis, dan ratio analysis**. Horizontal analysis melihat perubahan dari waktu ke waktu; vertical analysis mengubah setiap line item menjadi persentase dari base amount sehingga perusahaan berbeda ukuran menjadi lebih comparable; ratio analysis merangkum relationships tertentu untuk menilai **liquidity, profitability, dan solvency**. Chapter membahas 13 ratios utama, mulai current ratio hingga times interest earned, dan menekankan bahwa sebuah ratio tidak boleh diinterpretasikan sendirian tanpa trend, industry comparison, dan composition underlying accounts. Bagian terakhir berpindah dari reported net income menuju **sustainable income**: analyst harus memisahkan recurring operations dari unusual items, discontinued operations, serta components of comprehensive income untuk memperkirakan future performance. Untuk CF4, Chapter 18 merupakan salah satu source paling langsung bagi [[1.6 Financial Ratios and Interpretation]] dan juga memperkuat [[1.4 Company Account Structure]].

## 1. Why This Chapter Exists

Financial statements memberi raw data, tetapi raw data belum otomatis memberi answer.

Misalnya:

> Perusahaan melaporkan net income Rp500 miliar.

Apakah itu bagus?

Belum tahu.

Pertanyaan berikutnya harus muncul:

- tahun lalu berapa?
- sales-nya berapa?
- assets yang digunakan berapa?
- equity yang ditanamkan berapa?
- bagaimana dibanding competitor?
- income tersebut berasal dari continuing operations atau one-off gain?
- apakah company cukup liquid?
- apakah leverage terlalu besar?

Chapter 18 hadir karena financial analysis adalah proses **comparison + relationship + interpretation**.

Opening feature menggunakan Warren Buffett untuk menekankan bahwa successful investment analysis membutuhkan patience dan kemampuan membaca business fundamentals, bukan mengikuti market fad. Lesson utamanya:

> **Study the company, not only the stock price.**

Maka chapter membangun alur:

```text
Financial statements
↓
Comparative analysis
↓
Horizontal / Vertical / Ratios
↓
Liquidity / Profitability / Solvency
↓
Sustainable income
↓
Better judgment about future performance
```

> [!INFO] CF4 Connection
> Chapter ini sangat high-value untuk CF4 karena silabus secara eksplisit meminta kemampuan **menghitung dan menginterpretasikan financial/accounting ratios**.

## 3. Comparative Analysis

Financial analysis is fundamentally comparative.

A single number becomes meaningful when compared on one or more bases.

Weygandt highlights three common bases.

### 3.1 Intracompany Basis

Compare company with itself across periods.

Examples:

- 2017 vs 2016 sales;
- current ratio this year vs last year;
- five-year margin trend.

Question answered:

> **Is the company improving or deteriorating over time?**

### 3.2 Intercompany Basis

Compare one company with another company.

Examples:

- Macy’s vs another retailer;
- Company A ROA vs Company B ROA.

Question answered:

> **How does this company perform relative to a peer?**

### 3.3 Industry Averages

Compare company with industry norms.

Question answered:

> **Is performance typical, stronger, or weaker than companies facing broadly similar economics?**

### Why Comparison Matters

Suppose current ratio = 1.5.

Alone, that number is incomplete.

It becomes informative only after questions such as:

- last year 2.3 or 1.1?
- industry average 1.7 or 0.9?
- inventory quality good or obsolete?
- is business naturally low-working-capital?

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Ratio calculation ≠ analysis.**
>
> Calculation gives a number. Analysis requires comparison, cause, meaning, and limitation.

## 4. Horizontal Analysis

**Horizontal analysis** evaluates a series of financial statement data over a period of time.

Its purpose is to identify:

- trends;
- increases;
- decreases;
- relative rates of change.

Two outputs biasanya dihitung:

1. **amount change**;
2. **percentage change**.

### 4.1 Amount Change

$$
Amount\ Change
=
Current\ Year
-
Base\ Year
$$

### 4.2 Percentage Change

$$
Percentage\ Change
=
\frac{
Current\ Year-Base\ Year
}{
Base\ Year
}
\times 100\%
$$

Jika current year lebih kecil, hasilnya negative/decrease.

### Example — Current Assets

Current assets:

- 2016 = 180,000
- 2017 = 234,000

Amount increase:

$$
234{,}000-180{,}000
=
54{,}000
$$

Percentage increase:

$$
\frac{54{,}000}{180{,}000}
=
30\%
$$

Jadi current assets naik 54,000 atau 30%.

## 5. Horizontal Analysis of the Balance Sheet

Textbook menggunakan Quality Department Store untuk menunjukkan cara membaca perubahan structure perusahaan.

Contoh changes yang disorot:

- plant assets naik secara material;
- current liabilities naik;
- retained earnings naik besar;
- long-term liabilities relatif stable/down.

Interpretation textbook:

> perusahaan memperluas asset base dan sebagian besar membiayai expansion melalui retained income, bukan tambahan long-term debt.

Ini menunjukkan bahwa horizontal analysis tidak berhenti pada:

> "Plant assets naik 26.5%."

Analyst mencari **story behind the pattern**:

```text
Plant assets ↑ strongly
+
Retained earnings ↑ strongly
+
Long-term debt not rising comparably
↓
Expansion appears substantially internally financed
```

### Why It Matters

Balance-sheet trends dapat menunjukkan perubahan:

- asset mix;
- working capital;
- financing structure;
- leverage;
- investment intensity.

---

## 6. Horizontal Analysis of the Income Statement

Untuk income statement, pertanyaan penting adalah apakah:

- sales tumbuh;
- costs tumbuh lebih cepat atau lebih lambat;
- operating expenses terkendali;
- net income tumbuh lebih cepat dari sales.

Textbook's Quality Department Store illustration menunjukkan:

- net sales increase;
- COGS increase;
- operating expenses increase;
- gross profit increase;
- net income increase.

Jika:

$$
Sales\ Growth = 14.2\%
$$

sementara:

$$
Net\ Income\ Growth = 26.5\%
$$

maka profit tumbuh lebih cepat daripada sales.

Itu dapat mengindikasikan improved margins, cost leverage, atau favorable changes dalam other items.

Tetapi analyst harus inspect line items untuk mengetahui cause.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> Percentage column horizontal analysis **tidak additive**.
>
> Gross profit growth 17.1% bukan hasil menjumlahkan sales growth dan COGS growth.

Setiap line item's percentage dihitung terhadap **base amount-nya sendiri**.

---

## 7. Horizontal Analysis of Retained Earnings

Retained earnings berubah terutama melalui:

$$
Ending\ RE
=
Beginning\ RE
+
Net\ Income
-
Dividends
$$

Horizontal analysis retained earnings dapat menunjukkan apakah company:

- retain large share of earnings;
- increase distributions;
- finance growth internally.

Dalam Quality example, net income naik jauh lebih cepat daripada dividends sehingga ending retained earnings meningkat kuat.

Economic story:

```text
Higher earnings
+
Relatively modest dividend growth
↓
More earnings retained
↓
Greater internal financing capacity
```

---

## 8. Complications in Horizontal Analysis

Percentage calculations bisa misleading ketika base-year amount:

- sangat kecil;
- zero;
- negative.

### Base Near Zero

Jika base = 1 dan current = 10:

$$
Growth = 900\%
$$

Angka sangat besar tetapi economic size mungkin tetap kecil.

### Base Zero

Percentage change tidak meaningful karena denominator zero.

### Sign Change

Perubahan dari loss ke profit tidak selalu meaningfully expressed sebagai ordinary percentage growth.

> [!TIP] Reading Rule
> Jika percentage terlihat ekstrem, selalu lihat **absolute amount** dan base-year number.

---

## 9. Trend Analysis

Horizontal analysis dapat diperluas ke multiple periods dengan menetapkan base year = 100%.

Formula:

$$
Trend\ Percentage
=
\frac{
Current\ Year\ Amount
}{
Base\ Year\ Amount
}
\times 100\%
$$

Jika sales 2016 = 100 dan 2018 = 125, maka sales telah tumbuh 25% relatif ke base.

Trend percentages berguna untuk melihat whether components bergerak sejalan atau diverge.

Example reasoning:

```text
Sales index → 130
COGS index → 150
Expenses index → 160
```

maka costs tumbuh lebih cepat daripada sales, potential pressure on profit.

## 10. Vertical Analysis

**Vertical analysis** mengevaluasi financial statement data dengan menyatakan setiap item sebagai percentage dari base amount pada statement yang sama.

Tujuannya adalah normalization.

### Balance Sheet Base

Untuk balance sheet:

$$
Each\ Item\ \%
=
\frac{
Item
}{
Total\ Assets
}
\times100\%
$$

atau equivalent financing side percentages against total assets / total liabilities and equity.

### Income Statement Base

Untuk income statement:

$$
Each\ Item\ \%
=
\frac{
Item
}{
Net\ Sales
}
\times100\%
$$

Net sales = 100%.

---

## 11. Why Vertical Analysis Is Powerful

Suppose:

- Company A sales = 1 billion;
- Company B sales = 13 billion.

Absolute numbers difficult to compare.

After vertical analysis:

| Item | Company A | Company B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Net sales | 100% | 100% |
| COGS | 61% | 60% |
| Gross profit | 39% | 40% |
| Operating expenses | 17% | 30% |
| Net income | 12.6% | 5.3% |

Now scale disappears.

Textbook comparison between Quality Department Store and Macy’s demonstrates precisely this. Although Macy’s absolute net income jauh lebih besar, Quality's net income as percentage of sales materially higher.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Bigger company ≠ more profitable company on a relative basis.**

Absolute income measures scale.

Margin measures profit relative to sales.

---

## 12. Vertical Analysis of the Balance Sheet

Common-size balance sheet reveals asset and financing composition.

Example:

```text
Cash / Total Assets
Inventory / Total Assets
Plant Assets / Total Assets
Liabilities / Total Assets
Equity / Total Assets
```

Analyst can ask:

- is company more inventory-heavy?
- has debt become larger fraction of financing?
- are fixed assets expanding relative to total assets?
- is equity cushion increasing?

This is especially useful for companies of different sizes.

---

## 13. Vertical Analysis of the Income Statement

The income statement base is net sales.

Core relationships:

$$
Gross\ Profit\ Rate
=
\frac{Gross\ Profit}{Net\ Sales}
$$

$$
Operating\ Expense\ Percentage
=
\frac{Operating\ Expenses}{Net\ Sales}
$$

$$
Net\ Income\ Percentage
=
\frac{Net\ Income}{Net\ Sales}
$$

Vertical analysis helps diagnose where margin differences originate.

Example textbook logic:

Quality and Macy's gross-profit percentages are relatively close, tetapi operating income percentages differ substantially because selling and administrative expenses consume a much larger share of sales at Macy’s.

This illustrates causal reading:

```text
Similar gross margin
+
Different operating expense ratio
↓
Different operating profitability
```

---

## 14. Ratio Analysis

**Ratio analysis** expresses relationship among selected financial statement data.

A ratio dapat dinyatakan sebagai:

- percentage;
- rate/times;
- proportion.

Example:

If current assets = 13,626 and current liabilities = 3,926:

$$
Current\ Ratio
=
\frac{13{,}626}{3{,}926}
=
3.47
$$

Equivalent expressions:

- 347%;
- 3.47 times;
- 3.47:1.

Weygandt groups ratios into three major categories:

1. **liquidity**;
2. **profitability**;
3. **solvency**.

# LIQUIDITY RATIOS

## 15. Liquidity — What Question Are We Asking?

Liquidity asks:

> **Can the company meet near-term obligations?**

It focuses on:

- current assets;
- current liabilities;
- collectibility;
- inventory movement;
- short-term cash-generating ability.

Primary interested users:

- short-term creditors;
- suppliers;
- banks;
- management.

---

## 16. Current Ratio

Formula:

$$
Current\ Ratio
=
\frac{
Current\ Assets
}{
Current\ Liabilities
}
$$

### Meaning

Measures short-term debt-paying ability.

If:

$$
Current\ Ratio=2.0
$$

the company has $2 of current assets for each $1 of current liabilities.

### Working Capital

Related measure:

$$
Working\ Capital
=
Current\ Assets
-
Current\ Liabilities
$$

Weygandt notes current ratio often gives a more useful relative measure than absolute working capital.

### Why Composition Matters

Current assets may include:

- cash;
- receivables;
- inventory;
- prepaids.

A dollar of cash is more immediately available than slow-moving inventory.

Thus:

> **High current ratio does not automatically mean strong immediate liquidity.**

### Example Interpretation

Company current ratio = 1.52, industry = 1.70.

Conclusion should not be:

> "Company is bad."

More appropriate:

> liquidity appears somewhat below industry, but interpretation requires asset composition and business context.

---

## 17. Acid-Test (Quick) Ratio

Formula:

$$
Acid\text{-}Test\ Ratio
=
\frac{
Cash
+
Short\text{-}Term\ Investments
+
Net\ Accounts\ Receivable
}{
Current\ Liabilities
}
$$

### Why Exclude Inventory and Prepaids?

Because quick ratio focuses on **immediate short-term liquidity**.

Inventory:

- must first be sold;
- may be slow-moving;
- may be subject to markdown.

Prepaids:

- generally do not convert into cash.

### Current vs Quick

```text
Current ratio
includes all current assets

Quick ratio
includes only highly liquid current assets
```

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> Strong current ratio + weak quick ratio may indicate liquidity heavily dependent on inventory.

---

## 18. Accounts Receivable Turnover

Formula:

$$
Accounts\ Receivable\ Turnover
=
\frac{
Net\ Credit\ Sales
}{
Average\ Net\ Accounts\ Receivable
}
$$

Average:

$$
Average\ AR
=
\frac{
Beginning\ AR
+
Ending\ AR
}{2}
$$

### Meaning

Measures liquidity of receivables—how rapidly receivables are collected.

Higher turnover generally means faster collection.

### Collection Period

Related measure:

$$
Average\ Collection\ Period
=
\frac{365}{AR\ Turnover}
$$

If turnover = 10:

$$
Collection\ Period
=
36.5\ days
$$

### Interpretation

Compare with:

- company credit terms;
- historical trend;
- industry average.

If company turnover = 4.5 while industry = 12.5, potential collection problem deserves investigation.

But ratio alone does not prove poor credit management.

---

## 19. Inventory Turnover

Formula:

$$
Inventory\ Turnover
=
\frac{
Cost\ of\ Goods\ Sold
}{
Average\ Inventory
}
$$

Average inventory:

$$
Average\ Inventory
=
\frac{
Beginning\ Inventory+Ending\ Inventory
}{2}
$$

### Meaning

Measures how many times average inventory is sold/used during period.

### Days in Inventory

$$
Days\ in\ Inventory
=
\frac{365}{Inventory\ Turnover}
$$

Higher turnover generally means inventory moves faster.

But **too high** may indicate insufficient stock or lost sales.

Lower turnover may indicate:

- slow-moving goods;
- overstocking;
- obsolescence;
- weaker demand.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> Higher turnover is often favorable, but not automatically optimal.

---

# PROFITABILITY RATIOS

## 20. Profitability — What Question Are We Asking?

Profitability asks:

> **How effectively does the company generate income from sales, assets, and owners' capital?**

Interested users:

- shareholders;
- prospective investors;
- management;
- long-term creditors indirectly.

---

## 21. Profit Margin

Formula:

$$
Profit\ Margin
=
\frac{
Net\ Income
}{
Net\ Sales
}
$$

### Meaning

Net income generated by each sales dollar.

If:

$$
Profit\ Margin=8\%
$$

then each $1 sales produces approximately $0.08 net income.

### Interpretation

Higher margin may reflect:

- pricing strength;
- cost control;
- product mix;
- lower expenses.

But margins are industry-dependent.

A low-margin retailer may be highly successful through rapid asset turnover.

---

## 22. Asset Turnover

Formula:

$$
Asset\ Turnover
=
\frac{
Net\ Sales
}{
Average\ Total\ Assets
}
$$

Average assets:

$$
Average\ Assets
=
\frac{
Beginning\ Total\ Assets
+
Ending\ Total\ Assets
}{2}
$$

### Meaning

Measures efficiency in using assets to generate sales.

If:

$$
Asset\ Turnover=1.8
$$

each $1 average asset generates $1.80 sales.

### Interpretation

Higher generally indicates stronger asset utilization, but industry capital intensity matters.

---

## 23. Return on Assets

Formula:

$$
Return\ on\ Assets
=
\frac{
Net\ Income
}{
Average\ Total\ Assets
}
$$

### Meaning

Measures overall profitability of assets.

ROA combines:

- ability to generate sales from assets;
- ability to generate income from sales.

Conceptually:

$$
ROA
=
Profit\ Margin
\times
Asset\ Turnover
$$

because:

$$
\frac{NI}{Sales}
\times
\frac{Sales}{Average\ Assets}
=
\frac{NI}{Average\ Assets}
$$

### Interpretation

Two companies may reach same ROA differently:

```text
High margin × low turnover
or
Low margin × high turnover
```

This is an important mental model.

---

## 24. Return on Common Stockholders' Equity

Formula:

$$
ROE
=
\frac{
Net\ Income
-
Preferred\ Dividends
}{
Average\ Common\ Stockholders'\ Equity
}
$$

### Meaning

Measures profitability of common owners' investment.

### Why Subtract Preferred Dividends?

Preferred stockholders have prior claim on the portion represented by preferred dividends. Common shareholders' return should use income attributable to common ownership.

### Trading on the Equity

If company earns a return on borrowed/preferred capital greater than the financing cost, common shareholders may receive enhanced ROE.

Therefore:

$$
ROE > ROA
$$

can partly reflect financial leverage.

But higher ROE generated by more debt also means higher risk.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Higher ROE does not necessarily mean better operations.**
>
> It may be leverage-driven.

---

## 25. Earnings per Share (EPS)

Formula:

$$
EPS
=
\frac{
Net\ Income
-
Preferred\ Dividends
}{
Weighted\text{-}Average\ Common\ Shares\ Outstanding
}
$$

### Meaning

Measures net income earned on each common share.

### Why Weighted Average?

Shares outstanding can change during year.

Ending shares alone may not represent the capital base that existed throughout the period.

### Interpretation

EPS is widely watched, but by itself it does not measure return on investor cost or market valuation.

Example:

EPS = $5 does not tell you whether share price is $20 or $500.

---

## 26. Price-Earnings Ratio

Formula:

$$
P/E
=
\frac{
Market\ Price\ per\ Share
}{
Earnings\ per\ Share
}
$$

### Meaning

Shows how many dollars investors are willing to pay per dollar of current earnings.

If:

$$
P/E=20
$$

stock trades at 20 times EPS.

### Interpretation

Higher P/E may indicate market expectations of:

- growth;
- stronger earnings quality;
- lower perceived risk.

But not always.

Comparisons should consider:

- industry;
- growth;
- risk;
- earnings sustainability.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Higher P/E ≠ automatically better stock.**
>
> It means market attaches higher price relative to current earnings.

---

## 27. Payout Ratio

Formula:

$$
Payout\ Ratio
=
\frac{
Cash\ Dividends\ Declared\ on\ Common\ Stock
}{
Net\ Income
}
$$

### Meaning

Measures percentage of earnings distributed as cash dividends.

### Interpretation

High-growth companies often have lower payout ratios because they retain more income for expansion.

A mature company may distribute a larger proportion.

Thus:

> lower payout is not automatically bad; higher payout is not automatically good.

It reflects distribution and reinvestment policy.

# SOLVENCY RATIOS

## 28. Solvency — What Question Are We Asking?

Solvency asks:

> **Can the company survive and meet long-term obligations?**

Long-term creditors and shareholders focus on:

- debt burden;
- interest obligations;
- capital structure;
- earnings cushion.

---

## 29. Debt to Assets Ratio

Formula:

$$
Debt\ to\ Assets
=
\frac{
Total\ Liabilities
}{
Total\ Assets
}
$$

### Meaning

Measures percentage of total assets financed by creditors.

If ratio = 60%:

> approximately 60% of asset financing comes from liabilities.

### Interpretation

Higher ratio generally means:

- more leverage;
- less equity cushion;
- higher creditor risk;
- greater sensitivity to downturn.

But debt can also amplify shareholder returns when operations perform strongly.

---

## 30. Times Interest Earned

Weygandt formula:

$$
Times\ Interest\ Earned
=
\frac{
Net\ Income
+
Interest\ Expense
+
Income\ Tax\ Expense
}{
Interest\ Expense
}
$$

The numerator approximates income before interest and taxes:

$$
EBIT
=
NI+Interest+Tax
$$

so:

$$
TIE
=
\frac{EBIT}{Interest}
$$

### Meaning

Measures ability to meet interest payments as they come due.

If:

$$
TIE=6
$$

earnings before interest/tax cover interest expense six times.

### Interpretation

Higher TIE = larger earnings cushion.

Lower TIE = more vulnerability if operating profit declines.

### Limitation

TIE uses accounting earnings.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Earnings coverage ≠ cash coverage.**
>
> Profit can differ from cash flow.

---

## 31. Summary of the 13 Ratios

| # | Ratio | Formula | Main Purpose |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Current ratio | $\frac{Current\ Assets}{Current\ Liabilities}$ | Short-term debt-paying ability |
| 2 | Acid-test ratio | $\frac{Cash+ST\ Investments+Net\ AR}{Current\ Liabilities}$ | Immediate liquidity |
| 3 | AR turnover | $\frac{Net\ Credit\ Sales}{Average\ Net\ AR}$ | Receivables liquidity |
| 4 | Inventory turnover | $\frac{COGS}{Average\ Inventory}$ | Inventory liquidity |
| 5 | Profit margin | $\frac{Net\ Income}{Net\ Sales}$ | Profit per sales dollar |
| 6 | Asset turnover | $\frac{Net\ Sales}{Average\ Total\ Assets}$ | Asset-use efficiency |
| 7 | Return on assets | $\frac{Net\ Income}{Average\ Total\ Assets}$ | Overall asset profitability |
| 8 | Return on common equity | $\frac{NI-Pref.\ Div}{Average\ Common\ Equity}$ | Common-owner profitability |
| 9 | Earnings per share | $\frac{NI-Pref.\ Div}{WA\ Common\ Shares}$ | Earnings per common share |
| 10 | P/E ratio | $\frac{Market\ Price}{EPS}$ | Market price relative to earnings |
| 11 | Payout ratio | $\frac{Common\ Cash\ Dividends}{Net\ Income}$ | Earnings distribution |
| 12 | Debt to assets | $\frac{Total\ Liabilities}{Total\ Assets}$ | Creditor financing / leverage |
| 13 | Times interest earned | $\frac{NI+Interest+Tax}{Interest}$ | Interest-paying ability |

## 32. Why Average Balances Appear in Some Ratios

A recurring logic in chapter:

Income statement numbers measure a **period**.

Balance sheet numbers measure a **point in time**.

When combining them, average balance often gives a better denominator.

Thus:

```text
Sales / AR      → average AR
COGS / Inventory → average inventory
Sales / Assets   → average assets
NI / Assets      → average assets
NI / Equity      → average equity
```

Formula:

$$
Average\ Balance
=
\frac{
Beginning+Ending
}{2}
$$

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> Using ending balance instead of average can materially change turnover and return ratios when balances changed significantly during the year.

---

## 33. Ratios Must Be Interpreted Together

No single ratio tells the entire company story.

Example:

Company A:

- high current ratio;
- slow inventory turnover;
- weak AR turnover.

The high current ratio may simply reflect current assets trapped in inventory and receivables.

Another example:

- strong ROE;
- high debt-to-assets;
- low TIE.

ROE may be boosted by leverage while solvency weakens.

Mental model:

```text
Liquidity
+
Profitability
+
Solvency
+
Trend
+
Peer comparison
↓
More complete conclusion
```

---

## 34. Cause-and-Effect Reading of Ratios

### Case — Inventory Rises, Everything Else Constant

Current ratio:

$$
Current\ Assets\uparrow
\Rightarrow
Current\ Ratio\uparrow
$$

Quick ratio:

Inventory excluded:

$$
Quick\ Ratio\ unchanged
$$

Inventory turnover:

$$
Average\ Inventory\uparrow
\Rightarrow
Inventory\ Turnover\downarrow
$$

Lesson:

> one event can make one ratio look better and another worse.

Therefore financial analysis must consider economic substance.

---

## 35. Sustainable Income

After analyzing ratios, chapter turns to a different question:

> **Which part of current earnings is likely to continue?**

Company value depends on future cash flows.

Past earnings help forecast future performance only if analyst distinguishes recurring earnings from unusual noise.

**Sustainable income** is the most likely level of income to be obtained in the future.

Conceptually:

$$
Sustainable\ Income
\approx
Reported\ Income
-
Unusual/Nonrecurring\ Effects
$$

This is not a mechanical formula in the chapter. It is an analytical concept.

### Why It Matters

Suppose reported net income = 120.

Included:

- recurring operations = 90;
- one-time gain = 30.

For forecasting:

> 120 may overstate sustainable earnings.

---

## 36. Continuing vs Noncontinuing Components

Income statement helps analyst by separating:

- operating transactions;
- nonoperating transactions;
- continuing operations;
- certain unusual components.

Intermediate subtotals such as:

- income from operations;
- income before tax;
- income from continuing operations;

help identify underlying performance.

---

## 37. Discontinued Operations

A discontinued operation represents a component of business that has been disposed of or classified for disposal according to the source's criteria.

The chapter emphasizes separate reporting because it changes forward-looking interpretation.

Suppose company earned:

- continuing operations = 100;
- discontinued division = 25.

Net income = 125.

If the division no longer exists:

> analyst should not automatically forecast 125 next year.

More relevant starting point may be continuing earnings.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Net income ≠ sustainable income.**
>
> Future-oriented analysis often focuses more heavily on continuing operations.

---

## 38. Statement of Comprehensive Income

Weygandt introduces a broader performance measure:

$$
Comprehensive\ Income
=
Net\ Income
+
Other\ Comprehensive\ Income
$$

The statement includes net income plus certain items that bypass net income but affect equity.

Chapter identifies discontinued operations and other comprehensive income as important items to understand when assessing sustainable performance.

The key learning point is not that all OCI should be discarded.

Rather:

> analyst must understand whether an item belongs to recurring operating performance, unusual performance, or broader equity changes.

---

## 39. Sustainable Income and Future Cash Flows

The chapter's analytical bridge:

```text
Historical financial statements
↓
Reported income
↓
Separate recurring vs unusual items
↓
Assess sustainable income
↓
Estimate future earnings/cash flows
↓
Investment / credit decision
```

This explains why ratios and horizontal trends alone are insufficient.

A company can show:

- strong one-year net income growth;
- improving EPS;

while underlying continuing operations deteriorate if current period contains a large nonrecurring gain.

---

## 40. Quality of Comparison

Financial comparison is only useful when data are sufficiently comparable.

Potential issues include:

- different accounting methods;
- different fiscal periods;
- industry differences;
- one-off events;
- business-model differences;
- size differences;
- unusual acquisition/disposal activity.

Vertical analysis helps with **size**.

Horizontal analysis helps with **time trend**.

Ratios help with **relationships**.

But none automatically removes accounting or economic differences.

> [!TIP] Analyst Mindset
> When two ratios differ, do not immediately ask:
>
> "Which company is better?"
>
> First ask:
>
> "Why are they different?"

---

## 41. Integrated Example — Reading a Company as a System

Suppose Company X reports:

- sales +15%;
- net income +30%;
- current ratio 2.0 → 1.4;
- inventory turnover 8 → 5;
- ROA 6% → 8%;
- ROE 10% → 16%;
- debt/assets 40% → 58%;
- TIE 8 → 4.

### Step 1 — Horizontal Analysis

Sales and earnings improved.

Good initial signal.

### Step 2 — Liquidity

Current ratio falls.

Inventory turnover also falls.

Potential concern:

> more capital may be tied up in inventory while short-term cushion deteriorates.

### Step 3 — Profitability

ROA improves.

ROE improves even more.

### Step 4 — Solvency

Debt ratio rises sharply.

TIE falls.

Thus part of ROE improvement may be leverage-driven.

### Step 5 — Sustainable Income

Need to inspect whether 30% net-income growth includes unusual gains.

### Overall Interpretation

Not:

> "Company is clearly better because profit grew 30%."

More nuanced:

> operating profitability appears stronger, but liquidity and debt capacity weakened. Higher ROE may partly reflect leverage. Sustainability of earnings must be checked before concluding overall financial quality improved.

This is the thinking style the chapter is trying to build.

---

## 42. Common Misreadings the Chapter Helps Prevent

### Misreading 1

> Current ratio increased → company definitely healthier.

Not necessarily. Inventory or uncollectible receivables may have increased.

### Misreading 2

> Higher ROE → management definitely became more efficient.

Not necessarily. Leverage may have risen.

### Misreading 3

> Higher P/E → company has better accounting performance.

P/E is a market valuation relationship, not a pure accounting profitability measure.

### Misreading 4

> Higher inventory turnover always good.

Too high may mean inadequate inventory.

### Misreading 5

> Net income growth equals sustainable earnings growth.

Unusual/discontinued items may distort trend.

### Misreading 6

> Bigger net income means company is more profitable.

Absolute profit measures scale; margins and returns measure relative profitability.

## Chapter Synthesis

Chapter 18 can be understood as one story:

> **Financial statements only become useful after we create meaningful comparisons.**

First, horizontal analysis asks:

> How did the company change over time?

Then vertical analysis asks:

> What proportion of the statement does each item represent?

Then ratio analysis asks:

> What financial relationships reveal liquidity, profitability, and solvency?

Together:

```text
Horizontal
→ Direction through time

Vertical
→ Structure / composition

Ratios
→ Relationships / efficiency / risk
```

But analysis still cannot end there.

Reported income may contain components that will not persist. Therefore the final layer is:

```text
Reported results
↓
Recurring vs unusual
↓
Sustainable income
↓
Future-oriented judgment
```

The chapter therefore moves from **historical measurement** to **forward-looking interpretation**.

The most important habit is to avoid isolated conclusions.

A strong analyst combines:

- amount;
- percentage;
- trend;
- ratio;
- peer benchmark;
- composition;
- sustainability;
- economic context.

That is the difference between simply reading a financial statement and actually analyzing one.

## Quick Reading Review

- Financial analysis requires comparison, not isolated numbers.
- Three comparison bases: intracompany, intercompany, industry.
- Horizontal analysis measures change over time.
- Amount change:
  $$
  Current-Base
  $$
- Percentage change:
  $$
  \frac{Current-Base}{Base}
  $$
- Percentage changes are **not additive** across income-statement lines.
- Base-year zero makes ordinary percentage change meaningless.
- Vertical analysis normalizes each item to a common base.
- Balance sheet base = total assets.
- Income statement base = net sales.
- Common-size statements improve comparison across company sizes.
- Liquidity = short-term obligation capacity.
- Profitability = ability to generate earnings.
- Solvency = long-term debt-paying/survival capacity.
- Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities.
- Current ratio ignores quality/composition of current assets.
- Quick ratio excludes inventory and prepaids.
- AR turnover uses **net credit sales / average net AR**.
- Inventory turnover uses **COGS / average inventory**.
- Flow ÷ balance-sheet item usually suggests average denominator.
- Profit margin = net income / net sales.
- Asset turnover = net sales / average assets.
- ROA = net income / average assets.
- ROA can be viewed as margin × asset turnover.
- ROE uses income attributable to common / average common equity.
- ROE may exceed ROA because of leverage.
- EPS uses weighted-average common shares.
- P/E = market price / EPS.
- Higher P/E reflects market valuation expectations, not automatically superior accounting performance.
- Payout ratio measures earnings distributed as common cash dividends.
- Growth companies often retain more earnings and have lower payout.
- Debt/assets measures creditor-financed share of assets.
- TIE measures earnings coverage of interest.
- TIE is earnings-based, not cash-flow based.
- Higher/lower ratio is rarely meaningful without context.
- Sustainable income excludes/adjusts for unusual noise in reported income conceptually.
- Discontinued operations should not be blindly projected into future earnings.
- Net income and sustainable income are not identical.
- Comprehensive income is broader than net income.
- Strong analysis combines trend + structure + ratios + sustainability.
