# Robinson — Chapter 6: Understanding Cash Flow Statements

> [!ABSTRACT] Chapter in One View
> Chapter ini menjelaskan **statement of cash flows** sebagai laporan yang menunjukkan dari mana cash perusahaan berasal dan ke mana cash digunakan selama suatu periode.
>
> Jika income statement menjawab **“berapa accounting profit yang dihasilkan?”**, cash flow statement menjawab **“apa yang benar-benar terjadi pada cash?”**
>
> Perbedaan ini penting karena:
>
> $$
> Profit \neq Cash\ Flow
> $$
>
> Accrual accounting memungkinkan revenue diakui sebelum cash diterima dan expense diakui sebelum atau sesudah cash dibayar. Karena itu, perusahaan dapat melaporkan net income tinggi tetapi operating cash flow lemah.
>
> Cash flows diklasifikasikan menjadi:
>
> 1. **Operating activities (CFO)** — cash dari aktivitas utama yang menghasilkan revenue.
> 2. **Investing activities (CFI)** — acquisition/disposal long-term assets dan investments.
> 3. **Financing activities (CFF)** — transactions dengan providers of debt dan equity capital.
>
> Operating cash flow dapat disajikan dengan **direct method** atau **indirect method**. Direct method menunjukkan operating cash receipts/payments secara eksplisit. Indirect method dimulai dari net income lalu menyesuaikannya untuk non-cash items, non-operating items, dan changes in operating working capital.
>
> Core indirect-method logic:
>
> ```text
> Current operating asset ↑  → CFO ↓
> Current operating asset ↓  → CFO ↑
>
> Current operating liability ↑ → CFO ↑
> Current operating liability ↓ → CFO ↓
> ```
>
> Chapter juga menunjukkan bagaimana income statement dan comparative balance sheets dapat digunakan untuk reconstruct cash receipts dan cash payments, kemudian menganalisis earnings quality, common-size cash flows, FCFF, FCFE, dan cash-flow-based ratios.
>
> Untuk CF4, **Chapter 6 Sections 1–4 secara eksplisit termasuk referensi resmi Topik 1**, sehingga chapter ini merupakan core source untuk [[1.4 Company Account Structure]], [[1.5 Financial Statements Construction]], dan [[1.6 Financial Ratios and Interpretation]].

## 1. Why This Chapter Exists

Chapter 4 menunjukkan bagaimana perusahaan menghasilkan **accounting income**. Chapter 5 menunjukkan **resources dan claims** pada akhir periode. Chapter 6 menghubungkan keduanya dengan pertanyaan:

> **Apakah business tersebut benar-benar menghasilkan cash, dan bagaimana cash itu digunakan?**

Bayangkan perusahaan melaporkan revenue 1,000 dan net income 150. Sekilas terlihat kuat. Tetapi mungkin sebagian besar sales dilakukan on credit, accounts receivable naik tajam, inventory bertambah, company membeli machinery, debt jatuh tempo, dan dividends dibayar.

Net income 150 tidak memberi tahu apakah cash meningkat atau menurun.

Cash dibutuhkan untuk:

- membayar employees dan suppliers;
- membayar tax dan interest;
- melunasi debt;
- membeli productive assets;
- membayar dividends;
- melakukan acquisitions;
- bertahan ketika kondisi operasi memburuk.

Karena itu sources dan uses of cash membantu analyst menilai **liquidity, solvency, dan financial flexibility**.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Profit ≠ Cash Flow**, tetapi cash flow satu periode juga bukan complete measure of performance.
>
> Company dapat menghasilkan cash sekarang tetapi sekaligus menciptakan obligation yang dibayar kemudian. Maka profit, cash flow, dan balance sheet harus dibaca bersama.

> [!INFO] CF4 Connection
> `[CORE CF4]` Ini adalah extension langsung dari [[1.4 Company Account Structure]]: income statement memberikan accrual performance, balance sheet memberikan ending position, dan cash flow statement menjelaskan actual cash movement selama periode.

## 3. The Three Cash Flow Categories

Under both IFRS and US GAAP, cash flow statement uses three broad categories:

```text
Operating → running the business
Investing → changing the long-term asset/investment base
Financing → obtaining or returning capital
```

## 4. Operating Activities

Operating activities are the company's day-to-day activities that create revenue plus other activities not classified as investing or financing.

Typical operating cash inflows:

- cash sales;
- collections from customers;
- cash receipts from services;
- royalties;
- commissions;
- other operating receipts.

Typical operating cash outflows:

- payments to suppliers;
- salaries and wages;
- operating expenses;
- taxes;
- settlement of operating payables.

Cash flows related to **dealing/trading securities** are treated as operating activities in the source framework. This creates an important analytical rule:

> **Classification depends on economic purpose, not merely the name of the asset.**

## 5. Investing Activities

Investing activities generally involve purchasing and selling:

- property, plant and equipment;
- intangible assets;
- other long-term assets;
- investments in debt securities;
- investments in equity securities;
- certain loans/investments in other entities.

Examples:

```text
Buy machinery → Investing outflow
Sell machinery → Investing inflow
Buy securities as investment → Investing outflow
Sell investment → Investing inflow
```

### Economic Substance Matters

Jika restaurant menjual oven lama, oven tersebut adalah long-term operating asset, sehingga sale merupakan **investing activity**.

Tetapi jika oven manufacturer menjual oven kepada customer, oven tersebut adalah inventory/product, sehingga cash receipt merupakan **operating activity**.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Same physical item can have different cash-flow classification depending on its economic role.**

## 6. Financing Activities

Financing activities involve transactions with providers of capital.

Typical inflows:

- issuing debt;
- borrowing;
- issuing shares.

Typical outflows:

- repaying debt principal;
- repurchasing shares;
- distributions/dividends according to applicable classification;
- other returns of capital.

Financing section menjawab:

> **How did the company raise capital, and how much capital did it return?**

## 7. Non-Cash Investing and Financing Activities

Some important transactions change assets, liabilities, or equity without immediate cash movement.

Examples:

- purchasing an asset by issuing debt;
- acquiring an asset by issuing shares;
- converting debt into equity.

Karena tidak ada cash movement, transaksi ini **tidak dimasukkan sebagai cash inflow/outflow** dalam main cash flow statement. Mereka disajikan melalui supplementary disclosure sesuai applicable requirements.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Important transaction ≠ cash flow.**
>
> A transaction can materially change financial position without changing cash.

## 8. The Cash Reconciliation

Broad relationship:

$$
Ending\ Cash
=
Beginning\ Cash
+
CFO
+
CFI
+
CFF
+
Other\ Cash\ Effects
$$

Equivalent:

$$
\Delta Cash
=
CFO+CFI+CFF+Other\ Effects
$$

### Walmart Illustration

The source's Walmart example for fiscal 2010 reports approximately:

- CFO = 26,249;
- CFI = \(-11,620\);
- CFF = \(-14,191\);
- exchange-rate effect = 194.

Therefore:

$$
26{,}249-11{,}620-14{,}191+194=632
$$

Beginning cash:

$$
7{,}275
$$

Ending cash:

$$
7{,}275+632=7{,}907
$$

This illustrates the architecture:

```text
Operations generate cash
↓
Cash used for investment
↓
Cash raised/returned through financing
↓
Other effects
↓
Net change
↓
Ending cash
```

## 9. Direct vs Indirect Method

The direct-vs-indirect distinction applies to the **operating section**. Investing and financing sections remain cash-receipt/cash-payment based.

### 9.1 Direct Method

Direct method reports major operating cash receipts and payments directly:

```text
Cash received from customers
− Cash paid to suppliers
− Cash paid to employees
− Other operating cash payments
= Net cash from operating activities
```

It answers:

> **Exactly where did operating cash come from and where did it go?**

### 9.2 Indirect Method

Indirect method begins with net income and reconciles it to CFO:

```text
Net income
± non-cash items
± non-operating gains/losses
± working-capital changes
= CFO
```

It answers:

> **Why is operating cash flow different from accounting income?**

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Direct and indirect methods do not produce different CFO.**
>
> Mereka merupakan different presentation routes menuju operating cash flow yang sama.

## 10. Why Net Income and CFO Differ

Three major reasons drive the reconciliation.

### 10.1 Non-Cash Expenses

Examples:

- depreciation;
- amortization;
- depletion.

These reduce net income but do not create a current-period cash outflow from the expense recognition itself.

Therefore:

$$
Depreciation\ Expense
\Rightarrow
Add\ Back\ to\ NI
$$

### 10.2 Non-Operating Gains and Losses

Suppose equipment is sold and produces an accounting gain.

- cash proceeds belong in **investing**;
- gain is already included in net income.

Therefore under indirect CFO:

- gain on asset sale → subtract from NI;
- loss on asset sale → add back to NI.

This prevents investing effects from contaminating operating cash reconciliation.

### 10.3 Working-Capital Changes

Accrual accounting creates operating current assets and liabilities.

This is the heart of the indirect method.

## 11. Working-Capital Sign Logic

### Operating Current Assets

Examples:

- accounts receivable;
- inventory;
- prepaid expenses.

Rule:

$$
Operating\ Asset \uparrow
\Rightarrow
CFO \downarrow
$$

$$
Operating\ Asset \downarrow
\Rightarrow
CFO \uparrow
$$

Example: revenue 100 but AR increases 20.

Simplified cash collection:

$$
Cash\ Collected=100-20=80
$$

The income statement has 100 revenue, but only 80 has become cash.

### Operating Current Liabilities

Examples:

- accounts payable;
- wages payable;
- accrued expenses;
- taxes payable.

Rule:

$$
Operating\ Liability \uparrow
\Rightarrow
CFO \uparrow
$$

$$
Operating\ Liability \downarrow
\Rightarrow
CFO \downarrow
$$

If AP rises, company has recorded purchases/expenses but has not yet paid all related cash.

### Universal Memory Rule

```text
Operating Asset:
↑ subtract
↓ add

Operating Liability:
↑ add
↓ subtract
```

Or:

> **Operating assets move opposite CFO; operating liabilities move with CFO.**

> [!WARNING] Scope Trap
> Jangan menerapkan shortcut ini pada PPE, long-term debt, atau equity. Ini adalah working-capital logic, bukan rule untuk seluruh balance sheet.

## 12. Acme Corporation — Indirect Method

The textbook's Acme example combines all key adjustment types.

Net income:

$$
NI=2{,}210
$$

| Adjustment | CFO Effect |
|---|---:|
| Depreciation | +1,052 |
| Gain on sale of equipment | −205 |
| Increase in accounts receivable | −55 |
| Increase in inventory | −707 |
| Decrease in prepaid expenses | +23 |
| Increase in accounts payable | +263 |
| Increase in salary/wage payable | +10 |
| Decrease in interest payable | −12 |
| Increase in income tax payable | +5 |
| Increase in other accrued liabilities | +22 |

Thus:

$$
CFO=2{,}606
$$

Investing:

$$
CFI=762-1{,}300=-538
$$

Financing:

$$
CFF=-500-600-1{,}120=-2{,}220
$$

Net change:

$$
2{,}606-538-2{,}220=-152
$$

Beginning cash:

$$
1{,}163
$$

Ending cash:

$$
1{,}163-152=1{,}011
$$

### Lesson

Net income was positive and CFO was even larger, yet cash still declined because investing and financing uses exceeded operating cash generation.

Therefore:

> **Positive net income does not guarantee positive CFO.**
>
> **Positive CFO does not guarantee ending cash increases.**

## 13. Indirect Method Adjustment Table

### Additions to Net Income

Common textbook additions:

- depreciation;
- amortization;
- depletion;
- certain other non-cash charges;
- non-operating losses;
- decrease in current operating assets;
- increase in current operating liabilities;
- certain deferred-tax liability increases.

### Subtractions from Net Income

Common textbook subtractions:

- non-operating gains;
- increase in current operating assets;
- decrease in current operating liabilities;
- certain deferred-tax liability decreases;
- some non-cash income-type items.

Mental model:

```text
Net Income
↓
Remove non-cash effects
↓
Move non-operating effects to the correct section
↓
Reverse accrual-vs-cash working-capital differences
↓
CFO
```

## 14. Reconstructing the Direct Method

If only an indirect statement is published, analyst may reconstruct direct-format receipts and payments.

Robinson's logic:

1. **Disaggregate net income** into revenue and expense components.
2. **Remove non-operating and non-cash items.**
3. **Convert accrual revenue/expenses to cash receipts/payments** using balance-sheet changes.

```text
Income Statement
+
Comparative Balance Sheets
↓
Cash Receipts / Payments
↓
Direct-Format CFO
```

## 15. Cash Received from Customers

Simplified formula:

$$
Cash\ Received\ from\ Customers
=
Revenue-\Delta AR
$$

If AR decreases, \(\Delta AR\) is negative, so cash receipts exceed current-period revenue.

Example:

$$
Revenue=100
$$

$$
AR\ Increase=10
$$

then:

$$
Cash\ Received=100-10=90
$$

Shortcut:

```text
Revenue
+ decrease AR
− increase AR
= cash collected
```

## 16. Cash Paid to Suppliers

Two-step derivation.

### Step 1 — Purchases

Inventory relationship:

$$
Beginning\ Inventory+Purchases-COGS=Ending\ Inventory
$$

Therefore:

$$
Purchases=COGS+\Delta Inventory
$$

### Step 2 — Cash Payment

Accounts payable relationship:

$$
Beginning\ AP+Purchases-Cash\ Paid=Ending\ AP
$$

Thus:

$$
Cash\ Paid\ to\ Suppliers
=
Purchases-\Delta AP
$$

Combined:

$$
Cash\ Paid\ to\ Suppliers
=
COGS+\Delta Inventory-\Delta AP
$$

### Acme Example

$$
COGS=11{,}456
$$

Inventory increases 707:

$$
Purchases=11{,}456+707=12{,}163
$$

AP increases 263:

$$
Cash\ Paid=12{,}163-263=11{,}900
$$

Interpretation:

- Inventory ↑ → purchases exceed COGS → tends to increase cash need.
- AP ↑ → some purchases remain unpaid → reduces current cash payment.

## 17. Cash Paid for Operating Expenses

General intuition:

```text
Expense
+ increase prepaid asset
− decrease prepaid asset
− increase accrued liability
+ decrease accrued liability
= cash paid
```

### Why Prepaid Asset ↑ Raises Cash Paid

Company paid cash for a future benefit not yet fully expensed:

$$
Cash\ Paid>Expense
$$

### Why Accrued Liability ↑ Lowers Cash Paid

Company recognized an expense not yet fully paid:

$$
Cash\ Paid<Expense
$$

### Black Ice Example

Operating expense:

$$
30
$$

Prepaid insurance increases 4 and accrued utilities payable decreases 7:

$$
Cash\ Paid=30+4+7=41
$$

The payable decrease adds to payment because company settles prior accrued obligations in addition to current-period expense.

## 18. Cash Paid for Interest

Simplified formula:

$$
Cash\ Interest
=
Interest\ Expense-\Delta Interest\ Payable
$$

Acme:

- interest expense = 246;
- interest payable decreases 12.

Therefore:

$$
Cash\ Interest=246+12=258
$$

Again:

> **Expense measures accrual-period cost; cash paid measures settlement.**

## 19. Cash Paid for Taxes

Same accrual-to-cash principle:

$$
Cash\ Taxes
\approx
Tax\ Expense-\Delta Tax\ Payable
$$

with additional adjustments where deferred tax or other tax accounts matter.

Core CF4 lesson:

> **Tax expense ≠ cash tax payment.**

## 20. IFRS vs US GAAP Classification

`[TEXTBOOK CONTEXT]`

The source discusses differences in classification between IFRS and US GAAP.

One explicit example:

### Interest Paid

- **US GAAP:** operating cash flow.
- **IFRS:** may be operating or financing under the textbook framework.

The larger analytical lesson is that economically similar companies can report different CFO because of classification policy.

> [!WARNING] Comparability Trap
> When comparing CFO across companies, check:
>
> - accounting framework;
> - cash-flow classification policies;
> - consistency across periods.

A higher reported CFO can partly reflect classification rather than better underlying economics.

> [!IMPORTANT] Textbook Context
> This note preserves the accounting framework presented by the textbook instead of silently updating later standards.

## 21. Four-Step Cash Flow Analysis

A useful condensed framework from Robinson:

### Step 1 — Identify Major Sources and Uses

Ask:

- Is cash primarily generated by operations?
- Is cash being invested?
- Is financing providing or consuming cash?
- Did cash rise or fall?
- Is the pattern sustainable?

### Step 2 — Analyze CFO

Ask:

- What drives operating cash flow?
- Is it positive?
- Is it growing?
- Is it stable?
- Is CFO above or below net income?
- Why?

For a mature company, Robinson notes that it is generally expected and desirable for CFO to exceed net income because net income includes non-cash depreciation and amortization.

### Step 3 — Analyze Investing

Inspect:

- capital expenditures;
- acquisitions;
- securities investments;
- asset/business sales.

Ask:

> How much is being invested for the future, and where does the funding come from?

Negative CFI is not automatically bad: it may indicate productive investment.

Positive CFI is not automatically good: it may reflect asset/business sales.

### Step 4 — Analyze Financing

Inspect:

- new borrowing;
- debt repayment;
- share issuance;
- share repurchase;
- dividends.

Ask:

> Why is capital being raised or returned, and when will repayment be required?

## 22. CFO vs Net Income and Earnings Quality

Relationship between CFO and net income provides evidence about **earnings quality**.

Potential red flag:

```text
Net income strongly positive
BUT
CFO persistently weak
```

Possible drivers:

- accounts receivable rising;
- inventory absorbing cash;
- aggressive revenue recognition;
- accrual estimates;
- accounting income not converting to cash.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> One year of CFO < NI does **not** automatically prove manipulation.
>
> Analyst must examine cause, trend, business model, and persistence.

Conversely, CFO > NI can be a positive sign for mature firms, particularly where depreciation/amortization are material.

## 23. Groupe Danone Illustration

The source summarizes:

| € millions | 2008 | 2009 |
|---|---:|---:|
| CFO | 1,754 | 2,000 |
| CFI | -569 | 214 |
| CFF | -1,111 | -2,169 |
| Exchange effects | -31 | 8 |
| Increase in cash | 43 | 53 |

Interpretation:

- primary source of cash = **operations**;
- 2009 investing provided cash through sale of businesses/investments;
- financing consumed cash, especially repayment of liabilities and dividends.

Robinson highlights as favorable for a mature company:

1. operations are the primary cash source;
2. CFO exceeds net income;
3. CFO exceeds capital expenditures.

Mental narrative:

```text
Operations generate cash
↓
Cash funds investment needs
↓
Remaining cash can reduce debt / pay owners
```

## 24. Common-Size Cash Flow Statement

Robinson gives two approaches.

### Method 1 — Percentage of Total Cash Inflows / Outflows

For each inflow:

$$
\frac{Individual\ Cash\ Inflow}{Total\ Cash\ Inflows}
$$

For each outflow:

$$
\frac{Individual\ Cash\ Outflow}{Total\ Cash\ Outflows}
$$

This shows the relative importance of each cash source/use.

### Method 2 — Percentage of Net Revenue

$$
Common\text{-}Size\ Cash\ Item
=
\frac{Cash\ Flow\ Item}{Net\ Revenue}
$$

This scales cash flows against operating size.

### Direct vs Indirect Limitation

With direct method, operating receipts and payments are separately visible.

With indirect method, common-size analysis often has only **net CFO** rather than granular operating inflows/outflows.

## 25. Free Cash Flow — Basic Intuition

A company may generate positive CFO but require substantial capital expenditure.

Broad intuition:

$$
Free\ Cash\ Flow
\approx
CFO-Capital\ Expenditure
$$

Question:

> **How much operating cash remains after necessary reinvestment?**

The chapter then introduces **FCFF** and **FCFE**.

## 26. Free Cash Flow to the Firm — FCFF

FCFF is cash available to both **debt and equity capital providers** after operating costs, taxes, working-capital investment, and fixed-capital investment.

Starting from net income:

$$
FCFF
=
NI+NCC+Int(1-T)-FCInv-WCInv
$$

where:

- \(NI\) = net income;
- \(NCC\) = non-cash charges;
- \(Int\) = interest expense;
- \(T\) = tax rate;
- \(FCInv\) = fixed-capital investment;
- \(WCInv\) = working-capital investment.

Why add:

$$
Int(1-T)?
$$

Because net income is after interest expense, while FCFF belongs to both debt and equity providers.

Starting from CFO when CFO includes interest paid:

$$
FCFF=CFO+Int(1-T)-FCInv
$$

### Acme FCFF

$$
CFO=2{,}606
$$

After-tax interest:

$$
258(1-0.34)=170
$$

Net fixed-capital investment:

$$
1{,}300-762=538
$$

Thus:

$$
FCFF=2{,}606+170-538=2{,}238
$$

## 27. FCFF Classification Caveat

`[TEXTBOOK CONTEXT]`

Because IFRS may classify some interest/dividend cash flows differently, FCFF calculation from reported CFO must be consistent with what CFO already includes.

For example, if interest paid is classified in financing rather than operating, the analyst should not mechanically add after-tax interest as though CFO had already deducted it.

Core principle:

> **Adjust the formula to the reported classification; do not double-adjust.**

## 28. Free Cash Flow to Equity — FCFE

FCFE is cash available to common shareholders after operating needs, investments, and debt-financing effects.

Formula:

$$
FCFE
=
CFO-FCInv+Net\ Borrowing
$$

If company is a net debt repayer:

$$
FCFE
=
CFO-FCInv-Net\ Debt\ Repayment
$$

### Acme

$$
FCFE=2{,}606-538-500=1{,}568
$$

Positive FCFE means cash remains after required reinvestment and net debt repayment and is potentially available to common owners.

## 29. FCFF vs FCFE

| Feature | FCFF | FCFE |
|---|---|---|
| Available to | Debt + equity providers | Common equity holders |
| Core perspective | Firm-level | Equity-level |
| Interest | Neutralized appropriately | Debt financing already reflected |
| Net borrowing | Not equity-residual driver | Explicitly affects FCFE |

Mental shortcut:

```text
FCFF → cash for all capital providers
FCFE → residual cash for common equity
```

## 30. Cash-Flow Performance Ratios

### 30.1 Cash Flow to Revenue

$$
Cash\ Flow\ to\ Revenue
=
\frac{CFO}{Net\ Revenue}
$$

Measures operating cash generated per unit of revenue.

### 30.2 Cash Return on Assets

$$
Cash\ Return\ on\ Assets
=
\frac{CFO}{Average\ Total\ Assets}
$$

Measures operating cash generated per unit of asset investment.

### 30.3 Cash Return on Equity

$$
Cash\ Return\ on\ Equity
=
\frac{CFO}{Average\ Shareholders'\ Equity}
$$

Measures operating cash generation relative to owner investment.

### 30.4 Cash to Income

$$
Cash\ to\ Income
=
\frac{CFO}{Operating\ Income}
$$

Shows cash-generating ability relative to operating accounting income.

### 30.5 Cash Flow per Share

$$
Cash\ Flow\ per\ Share
=
\frac{CFO-Preferred\ Dividends}{Common\ Shares\ Outstanding}
$$

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Cash flow per share ≠ EPS.**
>
> EPS is accounting earnings per share; this metric is operating cash flow per common share.

## 31. Why Cash-Flow Ratios Matter

Traditional ratios may use:

- net income;
- EBIT;
- assets;
- liabilities.

Cash-flow ratios add:

> **How much actual operating cash supports reported performance and obligations?**

They are useful when accruals create large differences between earnings and cash generation.

But they still require:

- trend analysis;
- peer comparison;
- consistent classification;
- industry context;
- accounting-policy awareness.

## 32. Integrated Example

Suppose company reports:

- Net income = 150;
- Depreciation = 50;
- AR increases 80;
- Inventory increases 40;
- AP increases 30.

Approximate CFO:

$$
150+50-80-40+30=110
$$

Interpretation:

Depreciation adds back:

$$
+50
$$

Working capital consumes:

$$
80+40-30=90
$$

Net reconciliation effect:

$$
50-90=-40
$$

so:

$$
150-40=110
$$

This reveals the economics better than memorizing signs:

> earnings of 150 converted into only 110 of operating cash because working capital absorbed cash.

## 33. Common Classification and Sign Traps

### Trap 1 — Machinery Purchase = Operating Expense

Buying machinery is normally:

> **investing cash outflow**, not CFO merely because it costs money.

### Trap 2 — Debt Principal Repayment = Operating

Debt principal repayment is normally:

> **financing outflow**.

### Trap 3 — Gain on Asset Sale = CFO

Gain affects net income, but sale proceeds belong to investing. Indirect CFO removes the gain.

### Trap 4 — AR Increase Means More Cash

Usually the opposite:

$$
AR\uparrow \Rightarrow CFO\downarrow
$$

all else equal.

### Trap 5 — Inventory Increase Added to CFO

Inventory increase absorbs operating cash:

$$
Inventory\uparrow \Rightarrow CFO\downarrow
$$

### Trap 6 — AP Increase Means More Cash Paid

AP increase means some purchases remain unpaid:

$$
AP\uparrow \Rightarrow CFO\uparrow
$$

relative to accrual purchases/expenses.

## Chapter Synthesis

Chapter 6 changes the way financial statements should be read.

Income statement:

```text
Economic activity
↓
Accrual recognition
↓
Revenue / expenses
↓
Net income
```

Cash flow statement:

```text
Actual cash movement
↓
Operating
Investing
Financing
```

Indirect method bridges them:

```text
Net Income
↓
Remove non-cash effects
↓
Remove/reclassify non-operating effects
↓
Adjust working-capital accruals
↓
CFO
```

Then analyst asks:

```text
CFO
↓
Is it recurring and strong?
↓
Can it fund investments?
↓
What is happening in CFI?
↓
How is the business funded / distributing capital through CFF?
↓
Does cash generation support reported earnings?
↓
Liquidity / solvency / financial flexibility
```

The deepest lesson is not merely **“memorize CFO, CFI, CFF.”**

It is:

> **Understand the economic story connecting earnings, balance-sheet changes, investment, financing decisions, and actual cash.**

A company with high earnings but weak cash conversion may deserve scrutiny. A company with negative CFI may be investing for growth. Negative CFF may reflect debt repayment or capital return from strong operations. Positive CFF may fund profitable growth—or cover an operating cash shortfall.

Cash-flow interpretation therefore requires **classification + reconciliation + trend + business context**.

## Quick Reading Review

- Statement of cash flows shows **sources and uses of cash over a period**.
- Cash flows = **operating + investing + financing**.
- Operating = running the core business.
- Investing = long-term assets/investments.
- Financing = transactions with capital providers.
- Non-cash investing/financing activities are disclosed separately.
- Direct method shows actual operating receipts/payments.
- Indirect method reconciles **net income → CFO**.
- Both methods lead to the same CFO.
- Depreciation is added back because it is non-cash.
- Gain on asset sale is removed from CFO; sale proceeds belong in CFI.
- Operating asset ↑ → CFO ↓.
- Operating asset ↓ → CFO ↑.
- Operating liability ↑ → CFO ↑.
- Operating liability ↓ → CFO ↓.
- AR ↑ means revenue has not fully converted to cash.
- Inventory ↑ generally absorbs operating cash.
- AP ↑ means some purchases remain unpaid.
- Cash received from customers:
  $$
  Revenue-\Delta AR
  $$
- Purchases:
  $$
  COGS+\Delta Inventory
  $$
- Cash paid to suppliers:
  $$
  COGS+\Delta Inventory-\Delta AP
  $$
- Tax expense ≠ cash tax payment.
- Check IFRS/US GAAP classification before comparing CFO.
- Persistent high NI with weak CFO can be an earnings-quality warning signal.
- Negative CFI can represent productive investment.
- Positive CFI can arise from asset sales.
- Negative CFF can represent debt repayment/dividends/repurchases.
- Common-size cash flow can use total inflows/outflows or net revenue.
- FCFF:
  $$
  NI+NCC+Int(1-T)-FCInv-WCInv
  $$
- Or, when classification is consistent:
  $$
  CFO+Int(1-T)-FCInv
  $$
- FCFE:
  $$
  CFO-FCInv+Net\ Borrowing
  $$
- FCFF = cash for debt + equity providers.
- FCFE = residual cash for common shareholders.
- **Profit should be read together with cash conversion.**
