# Berk & DeMarzo — Chapter 26: Working Capital Management

> [!ABSTRACT] Chapter in One View
> Chapter 26 membahas **working capital management**—pengelolaan short-term assets dan short-term liabilities yang dibutuhkan untuk menjalankan operasi sehari-hari. Core accounts-nya adalah **cash, inventory, accounts receivable, dan accounts payable**.
>
> Working capital matters because capital tied up in these accounts memiliki **opportunity cost**. Cash yang tersimpan, inventory yang belum terjual, atau receivables yang belum tertagih adalah funds yang belum dapat digunakan untuk membayar debt, membiayai investment, atau dibagikan kepada shareholders.
>
> Chapter dimulai dengan **cash cycle** dan **cash conversion cycle (CCC)**:
>
> $$
> CCC
> =
> \text{Accounts Receivable Days}
> +
> \text{Inventory Days}
> -
> \text{Accounts Payable Days}
> $$
>
> Semakin panjang cycle, semakin lama cash firm terikat dalam operations. Working-capital reductions dapat langsung meningkatkan free cash flow dan firm value.
>
> Section 26.2 membahas **trade credit**, yaitu credit yang supplier berikan kepada customer. Terms seperti **2/10, Net 30** sebenarnya mengandung implicit financing rate. Tidak mengambil 2% discount untuk memperoleh tambahan 20 days financing berarti membayar periodic rate \(2/98=2.04\%\), yang setara dengan EAR sekitar **44.6%**. Karena itu firm harus membandingkan cost of trade credit dengan bank borrowing dan alternative financing.
>
> Trade credit tetap digunakan karena simple, flexible, low transaction cost, dan supplier kadang memiliki information/collateral advantage dibanding bank. Chapter juga membahas **collection float** dan **disbursement float**, yang memengaruhi timing actual cash availability.
>
> **Receivables management** dimulai dengan tiga policy decisions: credit standards, credit terms, dan collection policy. Setelah itu firm memonitor receivables menggunakan **accounts receivable days**, **aging schedule**, dan payments pattern. Aging schedule memberikan detail yang dapat disembunyikan oleh satu average collection-period number.
>
> **Payables management** adalah mirror image dari receivables management. Firm harus membayar **selambat mungkin tetapi tetap sesuai agreed terms**. Membayar terlalu cepat membuang opportunity return; terlambat berlebihan (**stretching accounts payable**) dapat menurunkan effective financing cost tetapi berisiko merusak supplier relationship, credit rating, dan bahkan menjadi unethical jika contract dilanggar.
>
> Inventory dibutuhkan bahkan dalam perfect markets karena merupakan factor of production. Firm menyeimbangkan benefit of avoiding **stock-outs** dan accommodating seasonal demand terhadap **acquisition, ordering, dan carrying costs**. **Just-in-time (JIT)** inventory berusaha menekan inventory balance mendekati zero, tetapi membutuhkan supplier coordination dan predictable demand.
>
> Section terakhir membahas **cash management**. Firms hold cash untuk tiga alasan: **transactions balance, precautionary balance, dan compensating balance**. Excess cash kemudian dapat ditempatkan pada money-market securities seperti Treasury bills, CDs, repurchase agreements, banker’s acceptances, commercial paper, dan short-term tax-exempt securities.
>
> Untuk CF4, Chapter 26 adalah source inti bagi [[2.3 Short and Medium-Term Finance]] karena membahas trade credit, receivables, payables, inventory financing requirements, cash balances, dan short-term money-market instruments.

# 1. Why This Chapter Exists

Long-term capital structure bukan satu-satunya financing problem perusahaan.

Firm harus setiap hari membiayai gap antara:

```text
Cash paid for inputs
        ↓
Production / inventory
        ↓
Sale
        ↓
Customer payment received
```

Cash dapat keluar jauh sebelum kembali.

Itulah alasan **working capital** dibutuhkan.

Main accounts:

- cash;
- inventory;
- accounts receivable;
- accounts payable.

Working-capital management berusaha:

> **menjamin operasi tetap berjalan tanpa mengikat terlalu banyak capital dalam short-term assets.**

Too little working capital:

- stock-outs;
- missed payments;
- liquidity problems;
- lost customers.

Too much working capital:

- excess inventory;
- slow receivables;
- idle cash;
- opportunity cost.

Chapter therefore focuses on optimization, not simply minimization.

# 26.1 Overview of Working Capital

## 3. What Is Working Capital?

Most projects require investment in **net working capital**.

Core components:

- cash required for operations;
- inventory;
- receivables;
- payables.

Important distinction:

> **Working cash ≠ excess cash.**

Working cash is needed for day-to-day operations.

Excess cash:

- is not needed to run business;
- can be invested at market rates;
- can conceptually offset debt in capital-structure analysis.

An increase in required net working capital:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta NWC>0
\Rightarrow
FCF\downarrow
}
\]

A reduction:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta NWC<0
\Rightarrow
FCF\uparrow
}
\]

---

# 4. The Cash Cycle

Typical operating sequence:

```text
Buy inventory
      ↓
Wait before paying supplier
      ↓
Hold / process inventory
      ↓
Sell product
      ↓
Wait for customer payment
      ↓
Receive cash
```

The **cash cycle** is the time between:

> cash payment for initial inventory  
> **and**  
> cash receipt from sale of resulting output.

---

# 5. Operating Cycle

The **operating cycle** begins earlier than the cash cycle.

It measures:

> average time between purchasing inventory and receiving cash from sale.

If inventory is purchased on credit:

```text
Inventory purchased
      ↓
Operating cycle begins

Supplier paid later
      ↓
Cash cycle begins
```

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Operating Cycle}
=
\text{Inventory Days}
+
\text{Accounts Receivable Days}
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Cash Cycle}
=
\text{Operating Cycle}
-
\text{Accounts Payable Days}
}
\]

---

# 6. Cash Conversion Cycle

Practitioners often use the **cash conversion cycle (CCC)**:

\[
\boxed{
CCC
=
AR\ Days
+
Inventory\ Days
-
AP\ Days
}
\tag{26.1}
\]

where:

\[
\boxed{
AR\ Days
=
\frac{\text{Accounts Receivable}}
{\text{Average Daily Sales}}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
Inventory\ Days
=
\frac{\text{Inventory}}
{\text{Average Daily COGS}}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
AP\ Days
=
\frac{\text{Accounts Payable}}
{\text{Average Daily COGS}}
}
\]

Interpretation:

```text
AR days ↑
→ cash tied up longer

Inventory days ↑
→ cash tied up longer

AP days ↑
→ supplier financing lasts longer
→ CCC falls
```

---

# 7. Negative Cash Conversion Cycle

Some firms receive cash from customers **before** they must pay suppliers.

Then:

\[
CCC<0
\]

Textbook examples include Amazon.com and Southwest.

A negative CCC means operating model itself can partly finance working capital.

Conversely, firms with very long production/sales cycles can require cash more than a year before revenue is received.

---

# 8. Working Capital and Firm Value

Any permanent reduction in required working capital releases cash immediately.

If firm reduces required NWC by:

\[
\$50,000
\]

permanently, it can distribute that amount immediately.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Permanent NWC Reduction}
\Rightarrow
\text{Equivalent Immediate Increase in Firm Value}
}
\]

subject to no offsetting operating consequences.

---

## 9. Example 26.1 — Costly Working Capital

Emerald City Paints requires:

\[
\$450,000
\]

working capital today.

It recovers the amount after:

\[
8\text{ years}
\]

Discount rate:

\[
6\%
\]

Cash flows:

\[
t=0:-450,000
\]

\[
t=8:+450,000
\]

NPV:

\[
NPV
=
-450,000
+
\frac{450,000}{1.06^8}
\]

\[
\boxed{
NPV=-\$167,664
}
\]

Although principal is fully recovered, time value of money makes working capital costly.

If improved inventory policy halves requirement:

\[
\text{Value Created}
=
\frac{167,664}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{
=\$83,832
}
\]

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Lesson
> Recovering working capital later does **not** make it costless. Capital tied up has time value.

# 26.2 Trade Credit

## 10. Accounts Receivable and Accounts Payable

If seller lets customer pay later:

Seller records:

> **Accounts Receivable**

Buyer records:

> **Accounts Payable**

The financing provided by seller to buyer is **trade credit**.

Trade credit is therefore:

> **a loan embedded inside a commercial transaction.**

---

# 11. Trade Credit Terms

### Net 30

\[
\text{Net 30}
\]

means full invoice is due within 30 days.

### 2/10, Net 30

Means:

- 2% discount if paid within 10 days;
- otherwise full invoice due at day 30.

```text
Day 0         Day 10                    Day 30
Invoice       Pay 98%                   Pay 100%
              if discount               if no discount
```

The customer effectively receives:

- free credit through day 10;
- then must pay implicit interest to delay payment until day 30.

---

# 12. Cost of Forgoing the Discount

For invoice price $100 with:

\[
2/10,\ Net\ 30
\]

Pay early:

\[
\$98
\]

Pay later:

\[
\$100
\]

Additional financing:

\[
\$98
\]

Interest:

\[
\$2
\]

Periodic rate:

\[
\boxed{
r
=
\frac{2}{98}
=
2.04\%
}
\]

for:

\[
30-10=20\text{ days}
\]

EAR:

\[
\boxed{
EAR
=
\left(
1+\frac{0.02}{0.98}
\right)^{365/20}
-1
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
EAR\approx44.6\%
}
\]

This is extremely expensive relative to many bank loans.

---

# 13. General Trade Credit Cost Formula

For terms:

\[
d/t_d,\ Net\ t_n
\]

where:

- \(d\) = discount rate;
- \(t_d\) = discount deadline;
- \(t_n\) = net payment date,

periodic cost of not taking discount:

\[
\boxed{
r_{\text{period}}
=
\frac{d}{1-d}
}
\]

financing period:

\[
\boxed{
N=t_n-t_d
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
EAR
=
\left(
1+\frac{d}{1-d}
\right)^{365/(t_n-t_d)}
-1
}
\]

---

## 14. Example 26.2 — 1/15, Net 40

Firm can pay:

\[
\$99\text{ on day 15}
\]

or:

\[
\$100\text{ on day 40}
\]

Extra financing period:

\[
40-15=25
\]

days.

EAR:

\[
EAR
=
\left(
\frac{100}{99}
\right)^{365/25}
-1
\]

\[
\boxed{
EAR=15.8\%
}
\]

Decision rule:

> If bank borrowing cost is below 15.8%, borrow from bank and take the supplier discount.

---

# 15. Benefits of Trade Credit

Trade credit can be attractive because it is:

### Simple

No separate loan paperwork may be required.

### Flexible

Used automatically as purchases change.

### Accessible

Sometimes it is the only financing available.

### Low Transaction Cost

It is embedded in ordinary purchases.

---

# 16. Why Suppliers Offer Trade Credit

If suppliers are not banks, why lend?

Textbook gives several reasons.

## 16.1 Indirect Price Discrimination

Supplier can offer favorable financing to selected customers instead of reducing price for everyone.

Example logic:

```text
List price unchanged
      ↓
Specific financing terms offered
      ↓
Effective price lower for target customer
```

---

## 16.2 Information Advantage

Supplier has ongoing commercial relationship and may know customer quality better than outside lender.

---

## 16.3 Enforcement Advantage

Supplier can threaten:

> **No payment → no future supplies.**

This can be powerful if inputs are critical.

---

## 16.4 Collateral / Resale Advantage

If customer defaults, supplier may seize inventory.

Because supplier operates in same industry, inventory can be more valuable to supplier than to a general outside lender.

---

# 17. Managing Float

There can be delay between “payment initiated” and “cash economically usable.”

This delay is **float**.

---

## 18. Collection Float

**Collection float** = time between customer payment and firm being able to use cash.

Three components:

### Mail Float

Time for payment to reach firm.

### Processing Float

Time to process and deposit.

### Availability Float

Time until bank credits funds for use.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Collection Float}
=
\text{Mail}
+
\text{Processing}
+
\text{Availability}
}
\]

Reducing collection float reduces working-capital requirement.

Electronic collection can potentially reduce the delay drastically.

---

## 19. Disbursement Float

**Disbursement float** = delay before a firm's supplier payment actually causes cash to leave the firm's account.

Firm may prefer longer disbursement float because cash remains available longer.

But excessive delay creates:

- late-payment penalties;
- damaged supplier relationship;
- ethical/contract issues.

Thus the goal is not “delay forever,” but:

> optimize timing while honoring obligations.

---

# 26.3 Receivables Management

## 20. Three Steps in Credit Policy

A firm must determine:

1. **credit standards**;
2. **credit terms**;
3. **collection policy**.

---

# 21. Credit Standards

Question:

> Who is eligible to buy on credit?

Restrictive standards:

- lower bad-debt risk;
- lower receivables;
- potentially lower sales.

Loose standards:

- higher sales;
- higher receivables;
- greater default risk.

Thus:

```text
Tighter credit
→ less sales + less receivables risk

Looser credit
→ more sales + more capital tied up + more credit risk
```

Large firms may assess credit internally; smaller firms may purchase external credit reports.

---

# 22. Credit Terms

After deciding who receives credit, firm defines terms:

- net period;
- discount percentage;
- discount period.

Example:

\[
2/15,\ Net\ 30
\]

is itself a financing policy.

Industry convention may influence small firms' choices.

---

# 23. Collection Policy

Firm decides what to do when customer pays late.

Possible escalation:

```text
Reminder
      ↓
Formal inquiry
      ↓
Late interest / penalty
      ↓
Legal threat / collection
```

Policy must balance:

- collection effectiveness;
- customer relationship;
- administrative/legal cost.

---

# Monitoring Receivables

## 24. Accounts Receivable Days

\[
\boxed{
AR\ Days
=
\frac{AR}
{\text{Average Daily Sales}}
}
\]

It measures average collection time.

If credit terms are:

\[
Net\ 30
\]

but:

\[
AR\ Days=50
\]

customers are paying:

\[
20\text{ days late on average}
\]

The firm should also monitor **trend over time** and industry/economic conditions.

---

## 25. Limitation of AR Days

AR days compresses everything into **one average**.

It can hide:

- seasonal effects;
- small group of severely overdue accounts;
- changes in payment distribution.

Therefore managers supplement it with an **aging schedule**.

---

# 26. Aging Schedule

An **aging schedule** groups receivables by days outstanding.

Example buckets:

- 1–15 days;
- 16–30;
- 31–45;
- 46–60;
- 60+.

It can be based on:

- number of accounts;
- dollar amount outstanding.

Textbook example has:

\[
AR=\$1.6m
\]

and average daily sales:

\[
\$65,000
\]

so:

\[
AR\ Days
=
\frac{1,600,000}{65,000}
\approx25
\]

days.

This average looks acceptable under Net 30.

But aging schedule reveals:

- 28% of customer accounts;
- 39% of dollars;

are actually late.

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Lesson
> **Average collection period can look healthy while a substantial subset of receivables is delinquent.**

---

## 27. Example 26.3 — FTS Aging Schedule

Financial Training Systems has:

| Days Outstanding | Amount |
|---|---:|
| 1–10 | $100,000 |
| 11–30 | $300,000 |
| 31–40 | $100,000 |
| 41–50 | $20,000 |
| 51–60 | $10,000 |
| 60+ | $2,000 |
| **Total** | **$532,000** |

Percentages:

| Days | % |
|---|---:|
| 1–10 | 18.8% |
| 11–30 | 56.4% |
| 31–40 | 18.8% |
| 41–50 | 3.8% |
| 51–60 | 1.9% |
| 60+ | 0.3% |

If aging schedule becomes more **bottom-heavy**—more balances migrate toward old buckets—the firm should revisit credit/collection policy.

---

# 28. Payments Pattern

A **payments pattern** measures what percentage of a month's sales is collected:

- in same month;
- one month later;
- two months later;
- etc.

Example normal pattern in textbook:

- 10% same month;
- 40% following month;
- 25% two months later;
- 20% three months later;
- 5% four months later.

Uses:

1. detect deterioration in collection;
2. forecast working-capital requirements.

---

# 26.4 Payables Management

## 29. Accounts Payable as Financing

Accounts payable are a source of short-term financing.

But firm should use trade credit only if:

> it is cheaper than alternative financing.

Higher discount percentage:

\[
\Rightarrow
\text{higher cost of forgoing discount}
\]

Shorter delay between discount and net dates:

\[
\Rightarrow
\text{higher annualized cost}
\]

---

# 30. Optimal Payment Timing

Textbook rule:

> **Pay on the latest day consistent with the option you choose.**

If taking discount under:

\[
2/10,\ Net\ 30
\]

pay on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Day 10}}
\]

not day 2.

If not taking discount:

\[
\boxed{\text{Pay on Day 30}}
\]

not day 16.

Why?

Every unnecessary early payment forfeits interest/opportunity return.

---

# 31. Accounts Payable Days Outstanding

\[
\boxed{
AP\ Days
=
\frac{\text{Accounts Payable}}
{\text{Average Daily COGS}}
}
\]

Compare with supplier terms.

If:

\[
AP\ Days>Net\ Date
\]

firm may be paying late.

If:

\[
AP\ Days<Net\ Date
\]

while not using discount, it may be paying too early.

---

## 32. Example 26.4 — Rowd Company

Average AP:

\[
\$250,000
\]

Average daily COGS:

\[
\$14,000
\]

Terms:

\[
2/15,\ Net\ 40
\]

AP days:

\[
\frac{250,000}{14,000}
=
17.9\text{ days}
\]

Firm forgoes discount.

This is poor management.

Why?

- paying near day 18;
- discount could be captured by paying day 15;
- if choosing not to capture it, firm should wait until day 40.

---

# 33. Stretching Accounts Payable

**Stretching accounts payable** means intentionally paying later than contractual due date.

Example:

\[
2/10,\ Net\ 30
\]

but firm pays day:

\[
45
\]

Instead of borrowing for:

\[
20\text{ days}
\]

after discount deadline, firm uses funds for:

\[
35\text{ days}
\]

Thus annualized financing cost falls.

But this does not make it automatically optimal.

---

## 34. Example 26.5 — Stretched Trade Credit

Terms:

\[
1/15,\ Net\ 40
\]

Firm delays until:

\[
Day\ 60
\]

Periodic rate:

\[
\frac{1}{99}=1.01\%
\]

Financing period:

\[
60-15=45\text{ days}
\]

Periods/year:

\[
\frac{365}{45}=8.11
\]

EAR:

\[
EAR
=
(1.0101)^{8.11}-1
\]

\[
\boxed{
EAR=8.49\%
}
\]

Lower than paying on day 40 because financing period is longer.

---

# 35. Costs of Stretching

Supplier can respond by:

- imposing COD (**cash on delivery**);
- requiring CBD (**cash before delivery**);
- discontinuing supply;
- offering worse future terms;
- damaging buyer credit reputation.

Firm may then need more expensive bank financing or inferior suppliers.

Textbook also highlights ethical concern:

> knowingly violating agreed payment terms is generally viewed as unethical business behavior.

Thus:

\[
\text{Lower explicit financing cost}
\]

may be outweighed by:

\[
\text{relationship + reputation + operating costs}
\]

---

# 26.5 Inventory Management

## 36. Why Inventory Is Different

Under perfect markets:

- payables/receivables could theoretically be replaced by market financing.

But firms still need inventory because:

> inventory is an actual factor of production.

Therefore inventory management remains economically relevant even if financing markets are frictionless.

---

# 37. Benefit 1 — Avoid Stock-Outs

**Stock-out** = firm runs out of needed goods/input.

Consequences:

- lost sales;
- production interruption;
- dissatisfied customers;
- customers switching competitors.

Holding inventory reduces this risk.

---

# 38. Benefit 2 — Match Seasonal Demand and Production Efficiency

Demand may be seasonal while efficient production is relatively smooth.

Textbook example: Sandpoint Toy Company.

About 80% of annual sales occur September–December.

Two extremes:

### Constant Production

Pros:

- efficient steady operations.

Cons:

- large inventory buildup;
- capital tied up.

### Seasonal Production

Pros:

- less inventory.

Cons:

- overtime / wear;
- seasonal hiring;
- training costs;
- capacity strain.

Optimal policy is often a compromise.

---

# 39. Costs of Holding Inventory

Three direct categories:

### Acquisition Costs

Cost of inventory purchased.

### Order Costs

Costs of placing and receiving orders.

### Carrying Costs

Include:

- storage;
- insurance;
- taxes;
- spoilage;
- obsolescence;
- opportunity cost of funds.

Trade-off:

```text
Less inventory
      ↓
Lower carrying cost
but
More frequent orders
      ↓
Higher order cost
```

---

# 40. Working-Capital Benefit of Lower Inventory

Inventory reduction releases cash.

Textbook example:

GAP reduced inventory days by 24% in 2003 and freed:

\[
\$344\text{ million}
\]

for other uses.

This is another practical illustration of:

\[
Inventory\downarrow
\Rightarrow
NWC\downarrow
\Rightarrow
FCF\uparrow
\]

provided customer service and production are not harmed.

`[TEXTBOOK CONTEXT]` Company figures are historical examples.

---

# 41. Just-in-Time Inventory

**Just-in-time (JIT)** inventory management attempts to acquire inventory exactly when needed so inventory balance stays zero or near zero.

Benefits:

- lower carrying cost;
- less capital tied up;
- less obsolete inventory.

Requirements:

- exceptional supplier coordination;
- reliable delivery;
- predictable demand.

Risk:

> disruption can cause immediate stock-outs because buffer inventory is minimal.

---

# 26.6 Cash Management

## 42. Why Hold Cash?

In MM perfect market:

- firm can raise funds instantly at fair price;
- excess cash can be invested at fair return.

So exact cash balance would be irrelevant.

Real markets create:

- transaction costs;
- financing delays;
- uncertain cash needs;
- tax costs of excess cash;
- liquidity risk.

Thus firms hold cash intentionally.

Textbook gives three motivations:

1. transactions balance;
2. precautionary balance;
3. compensating balance.

---

# 43. Transactions Balance

**Transactions balance** = cash held to meet routine bills and day-to-day payments.

Its required size depends on:

- size/frequency of transactions;
- cash cycle;
- near-term liabilities.

### Quick Ratio

Textbook mentions:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Quick Ratio}
=
\frac{\text{Current Assets}-\text{Inventory}}
{\text{Current Liabilities}}
}
\]

as common liquidity indicator.

Holding more cash raises quick ratio.

---

# 44. Precautionary Balance

**Precautionary balance** = cash held to buffer uncertainty in future cash needs.

Greater operating cash-flow volatility:

\[
\Rightarrow
\text{larger precautionary balance}
\]

Firm may hold liquid assets equal to a multiple of observed cash-flow volatility to reduce cash-shortfall probability.

---

# 45. Compensating Balance

A **compensating balance** is minimum deposit bank requires in exchange for services.

It may:

- earn no interest;
- earn very low interest.

Economically, part of the firm's cash is unavailable for alternative use.

Thus compensating balance has an opportunity cost.

---

# 46. Firm Characteristics and Cash Holdings

Textbook notes:

### Higher Cash Holdings

Often associated with:

- risky firms;
- firms with high growth opportunities.

### Lower Cash Holdings

Often associated with:

- firms with easy access to capital markets;
- low transaction cost of raising cash.

Intuition:

```text
Hard/expensive to raise emergency cash
→ hold more cash today
```

# Alternative Short-Term Investments

## 47. Money-Market Securities

Excess short-term cash can be invested in **money-market securities**.

Textbook Table 26.3 includes:

1. Treasury bills;
2. Certificates of Deposit;
3. Repurchase Agreements;
4. Banker’s Acceptances;

## 48. Treasury Bills

Short-term U.S. government debt.

Textbook characterizes:

- very low/default-free credit risk in U.S. context;
- very liquid;
- short maturity.

Firm choosing minimal credit risk prefers Treasury bills.

---

## 49. Certificates of Deposit

**Certificates of Deposit (CDs)** are short-term bank debt.

Textbook institutional details:

- large denomination;
- maturities up to roughly one year;
- some secondary market liquidity.

Credit risk depends on bank and deposit-insurance coverage.

`[TEXTBOOK CONTEXT]` Exact FDIC limits and denominations are period-specific.

---

## 50. Repurchase Agreements

A **repurchase agreement (repo)** is economically a short-term collateralized loan.

Structure:

```text
Dealer sells security today
      ↓
Investor provides cash
      ↓
Dealer agrees to repurchase later
at higher price
```

Difference between repurchase and sale price is financing return.

Typically:

- very short-term;
- collateralized;
- little but not zero counterparty risk.

---

## 51. Banker’s Acceptances

A **banker’s acceptance** is a draft:

- written by borrower;
- guaranteed by a bank.

Common textbook use:

> international trade.

Because both borrower and bank stand behind payment, default risk is generally low.

Holder may:

- keep until maturity;
- sell before maturity at discount.

---

## 52. Commercial Paper

**Commercial paper** = short-term unsecured debt issued by large corporations.

Characteristics from source:

- typically short maturity;
- issuer credit quality determines default risk;
- no active secondary market in textbook treatment;
- issuer may repurchase.

This directly links Chapter 26 to [[2.3 Short and Medium-Term Finance]].

---

## 53. Short-Term Tax Exempts

Textbook discusses short-term state/local-government debt whose interest receives U.S. federal tax preference.

Because of favorable tax treatment:

> pretax yield can be lower than comparable taxable security.

`[TEXTBOOK CONTEXT]` Jurisdiction-specific tax treatment is not durable CF4 law.

---

# 54. Risk–Liquidity–Return Trade-Off

Money-market choices differ in:

- default risk;
- liquidity;
- maturity;
- expected yield.

General trade-off:

```text
Safer + more liquid
      ↓
Lower expected return

More risk / less liquidity
      ↓
Higher expected return
```

Financial manager must match investment to likely timing of cash need.

If funds may be needed within 30 days:

> illiquid securities are less suitable.

# Chapter Synthesis

Chapter 26 is fundamentally about **time**.

Corporate operations create timing gaps:

```text
Buy
Produce
Sell
Collect
Pay
```

Each delay has financing implications.

Inventory ties up cash before sale.

Receivables delay cash receipt after sale.

Payables delay cash payment to suppliers and therefore provide financing.

The result is summarized by:

\[
CCC
=
AR\ Days
+
Inventory\ Days
-
AP\ Days
\]

A longer CCC means firm must finance operations for longer.

That gives the chapter's first major principle:

> **Working capital is not accounting clutter—it is an investment of capital.**

Even if full inventory or receivables are eventually converted back to cash, firm loses time value while capital is tied up.

The second principle is:

> **Commercial terms are financing terms.**

A supplier discount like 2/10, Net 30 contains an implicit interest rate. Forgoing apparently small discount can imply extremely high annualized borrowing cost.

The third principle is:

> **Optimize, do not mechanically minimize.**

Examples:

- low receivables can mean restrictive credit and lost sales;
- low inventory can create stock-outs;
- high payables can damage suppliers;
- low cash can create liquidity crises.

Efficient management finds the point where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

The fourth principle is:

> **Measure working-capital quality with more than one aggregate ratio.**

Accounts receivable days can hide overdue customers, so firms use aging schedules and payment patterns. Payables days must be compared with contractual terms. Inventory days must be understood relative to production and seasonal demand.

Finally, cash management recognizes that liquidity itself is valuable in imperfect markets. Firms hold transactions, precautionary, and compensating balances and invest temporary excess cash according to their required mix of:

```text
Safety
Liquidity
Return
```

The entire chapter can be condensed into:

```text
OPERATING TIMING
      ↓
AR + INVENTORY - AP
      ↓
CASH CONVERSION CYCLE
      ↓
CAPITAL TIED UP
      ↓
OPPORTUNITY COST
      ↓
FREE CASH FLOW
      ↓
FIRM VALUE
```

# Quick Reading Review

- Working capital consists of short-term operating assets/liabilities.
- Working cash is different from excess cash.
- Increase in NWC is an investment and reduces FCF.
- Operating cycle:
  \[
  Inventory\ Days+AR\ Days
  \]
- Cash conversion cycle:
  \[
  CCC=AR\ Days+Inventory\ Days-AP\ Days
  \]
- Negative CCC means customers can pay before firm pays suppliers.
- Permanently reducing NWC releases cash and increases firm value.
- Trade credit = supplier loan embedded in purchase.
- 2/10, Net 30 = 2% discount through day 10, otherwise full payment day 30.
- Cost of forgoing discount:
  \[
  r=\frac{d}{1-d}
  \]
  over:
  \[
  t_n-t_d
  \]
  days.
- General EAR:
  \[
  EAR=
  \left(1+\frac{d}{1-d}\right)^{365/(t_n-t_d)}-1
  \]
- 2/10, Net 30 implies approximately 44.6% EAR.
- Trade credit can be simple, flexible, and sometimes uniquely available.
- Suppliers can have better information and enforcement ability than banks.
- Collection float = mail + processing + availability delays.
- Receivables policy: **credit standards → credit terms → collection policy**.
- AR days measures average collection time.
- Aging schedule exposes overdue balances hidden by average AR days.
- AP should generally be paid on the **latest permitted day** consistent with chosen discount strategy.
- AP days:
  \[
  AP/\text{Average Daily COGS}
  \]
- Stretching AP can reduce annualized financing cost but damage supplier relationships and reputation.
- Inventory prevents stock-outs and allows production smoothing.
- Inventory cost categories: acquisition, ordering, carrying.
- JIT minimizes inventory but requires reliable supply and demand.
- Firms hold cash for transactions, precaution, and compensating balances.
- Quick ratio:
  \[
  \frac{CA-Inventory}{CL}
  \]
- Temporary cash can be invested in Treasury bills, CDs, repos, banker’s acceptances, commercial paper, and short-term tax-exempt securities.
- Short-term investment decision trades off **risk, liquidity, and return**.
- Fastest Chapter 26 mental model:
  **Time → Working Capital → Financing Need → Opportunity Cost → FCF → Firm Value.**
