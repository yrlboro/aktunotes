# Brigham — Chapter 14: Distributions to Shareholders: Dividends and Repurchases

> [!ABSTRACT] Chapter in One View
> Chapter 14 membahas **distribution policy**: setelah perusahaan menghasilkan *free cash flow* (FCF), berapa banyak cash yang seharusnya dikembalikan kepada shareholders, apakah melalui **cash dividends** atau **stock repurchases**, dan seberapa stabil distributions tersebut sebaiknya dibuat.
>
> Logika chapter dimulai dari sumber cash. FCF tersedia bagi investors setelah operating expenses, taxes, dan required investment in operating capital. Cash kemudian dapat digunakan untuk interest, principal repayment, dividends, share repurchases, atau financial investments. Setelah capital structure dan liquidity needs dipertimbangkan, residual cash dapat didistribusikan kepada shareholders.
>
> Brigham kemudian bertanya apakah bentuk distribution dapat memengaruhi firm value. Tiga classical views dibandingkan: **dividend irrelevance theory**, **dividend preference / bird-in-the-hand theory**, dan **tax-effect theory**. Dalam perfect-market MM world, payout form tidak mengubah value; dalam dunia nyata, taxes, transaction costs, agency problems, investor preferences, dan information asymmetry membuat payout policy potentially relevant.
>
> Dividend announcements juga mempunyai **information content**. Karena firms enggan memotong regular dividend, dividend increase dapat dibaca sebagai signal bahwa management percaya higher earnings/cash flows dapat dipertahankan; dividend cut sering membawa negative signal. Inilah salah satu alasan regular dividends cenderung **sticky** dan firms menyukai stable dividend policy.
>
> Chapter juga menunjukkan bahwa dividend dan repurchase dapat menjadi economically equivalent dalam simplified setting. Dividend mengurangi cash dan equity value serta menyebabkan stock price turun kira-kira sebesar dividend per share. Repurchase mengurangi cash dan total equity value sekaligus mengurangi shares outstanding; pada intrinsic-value logic textbook, **repurchase itself tidak mengubah per-share stock price**. Aggregate shareholder wealth sama sebelum dan sesudah distribution jika taxes, signaling, dan market imperfections diabaikan.
>
> Tetapi dalam practice, repurchases memberikan flexibility, choice kepada shareholders, dan dapat digunakan untuk one-time excess cash, capital-structure changes, serta employee stock-option needs. Dividends lebih predictable dan cocok bagi investors yang menginginkan regular income. Brigham karena itu condong pada practical policy berupa **sustainable regular dividend + flexible repurchases for residual cash**.
>
> Untuk CF4, core Brigham Chapter 14 adalah **§§14.1–14.3, 14.5–14.6, 14.9–14.10** dan terutama mendukung [[3.2 Sources of Finance and Capital Structure]] melalui pembahasan dividend/distribution policy.

## 1. Why This Chapter Exists

Mature companies berbeda dari high-growth companies dalam penggunaan cash.

High-growth firms biasanya memiliki banyak positive-NPV investment opportunities, sehingga cash yang dihasilkan lebih banyak direinvestasikan.

Mature firms dengan stable cash flows tetapi limited growth opportunities cenderung menghasilkan **excess free cash flow**. Pertanyaan berikutnya menjadi:

> **Apa yang harus dilakukan management dengan cash yang tidak lagi dibutuhkan untuk valuable operating investments?**

Opening Microsoft illustration menunjukkan corporate life-cycle logic:

```text
High growth
↓
many investment opportunities
↓
retain most cash
↓
little/no shareholder distribution

Growth slows
↓
FCF becomes large and positive
↓
fewer high-return reinvestment opportunities
↓
dividends and/or repurchases increase
```

Brigham menekankan tiga dimensions dari **distribution policy**:

1. **level of distributions** — berapa banyak cash yang dibagikan;
2. **form of distributions** — dividends versus repurchases;
3. **stability of distributions** — seberapa predictable payout tersebut.

Underlying objective tetap sama seperti chapter corporate finance lain:

> **maximize intrinsic shareholder value.**

Karena itu distribution policy tidak boleh dilihat sebagai keputusan terpisah. Ia berinteraksi dengan:

- investment opportunities;
- capital structure;
- working-capital/liquidity policy;
- taxes;
- investor preferences;
- information conveyed to the market.

## 3. 14.1 An Overview of Cash Distributions

`[CORE CF4]`

### Free Cash Flow Is the Starting Point

Brigham menggunakan FCF sebagai cash yang tersedia untuk investors setelah:

- operating expenses;
- taxes;
- required investment in operating capital.

Secara conceptual:

$$
FCF
=
NOPAT
-
\text{Investment in Operating Capital}
$$

Karena growth membutuhkan investment in operating capital, perusahaan yang profitable tetap dapat memiliki:

$$
FCF<0
$$

jika growth dan reinvestment needs sangat besar.

Sebaliknya, ketika growth melambat dan operating profitability tetap kuat:

$$
FCF>0
$$

dan distribution capacity meningkat.

### Five Uses of Free Cash Flow

Brigham menyebut lima potentially “good” uses:

1. pay interest;
2. repay debt principal;
3. pay dividends;
4. repurchase stock;
5. purchase non-operating/financial assets such as marketable securities.

Mental model chapter:

```text
Operations
↓
Free Cash Flow
├── Interest
├── Net debt repayment / financing effects
├── Dividends
├── Share repurchases
└── Marketable securities
```

### Capital Structure Interaction

Capital structure policy determines:

- interest obligations;
- target amount of debt;
- whether debt tends to rise or fall as company value grows.

Brigham notes bahwa mature firms tidak otomatis “pay all debt off” ketika FCF positif. Jika company wants to maintain a target debt ratio, debt may rise with firm value.

Because interest provides a tax shield in the textbook framework, eliminating all debt could sacrifice financing benefits.

### Marketable Securities as a Buffer

Holding financial assets provides:

**Benefits**

- liquidity during downturns;
- protection against financial distress;
- funding reserve if attractive investments appear unexpectedly;
- avoids immediate flotation/signaling costs of external financing.

**Costs / agency risk**

- managers may waste excess liquidity;
- acquisitions or perks may destroy value.

Thus cash balances are not automatically “safe value.” Large unused cash pools can create agency problems.

### Interdependence of Policies

Distribution policy is the residual outcome of several linked decisions:

```text
Investment opportunities
↓
FCF

Capital structure policy
↓
interest + debt financing needs

Working-capital policy
↓
target liquidity / marketable securities

Remaining cash
↓
shareholder distributions
```

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Profit ≠ distributable free cash flow.**
>
> A profitable high-growth company may need so much reinvestment that little cash is available for shareholders.

## 4. 14.2 Procedures for Cash Distributions

`[CORE CF4]`

Companies distribute cash primarily through:

- **cash dividends**;
- **stock repurchases**.

---

### 4.1 Dividend Payment Procedures

Brigham describes four key dates.

#### 1. Declaration Date

Board formally declares dividend.

At that point:

- dividend becomes a corporate obligation;
- retained earnings falls;
- a dividend payable/current liability is recognized in textbook accounting description.

#### 2. Holder-of-Record Date

Company identifies shareholders officially entitled to receive the dividend.

#### 3. Ex-Dividend Date

Investor buying on or after the ex-dividend date does **not** receive the upcoming dividend.

Under the settlement convention described in this edition, ex-dividend date occurs two business days before record date.

> [!CAUTION] Textbook Context
> Settlement conventions can change over time. For CF4 condensed-book purposes, preserve the textbook mechanism and focus on the conceptual relation among declaration, ex-dividend, record, and payment dates.

#### 4. Payment Date

Cash is actually distributed to shareholders of record.

### Price Around the Ex-Dividend Date

Ignoring unrelated market movements and taxes, stock price is expected to fall approximately by the dividend amount when the stock goes ex-dividend.

Conceptually:

$$
P_{\text{ex}}
\approx
P_{\text{cum}}
-
D
$$

The firm has distributed cash, so intrinsic equity value has fallen by the same amount.

---

### 4.2 Stock Repurchase Procedures

A stock repurchase occurs when the company buys its own outstanding shares.

Brigham identifies common motivations:

1. recapitalization / increasing leverage;
2. acquiring shares for employee stock-option exercises;
3. distributing excess cash.

### Three Main Repurchase Methods

#### Open-Market Repurchase

Company buys shares through brokers in the public market.

#### Tender Offer

Company offers to purchase a stated quantity of shares at a specified price during a limited period.

If more shares are tendered than desired, purchases may be made pro rata.

#### Targeted Repurchase

Company negotiates directly to buy a block from a specific large shareholder.

### Treasury Stock

Repurchased shares become **treasury stock** in the textbook accounting presentation and reduce outstanding shares/common equity.

---

### 4.3 Distribution Patterns

Brigham notes a historical shift:

- total distributions relative to earnings were relatively stable in the period discussed;
- the mix shifted away from cash dividends and toward repurchases;
- repurchases became increasingly important for large companies.

The exact historical percentages are `[TEXTBOOK CONTEXT]`; the durable lesson is:

> **Modern payout policy uses both dividends and repurchases rather than treating dividends as the only cash-distribution mechanism.**

## 5. 14.3 Cash Distributions and Firm Value

`[CORE CF4]`

The central question:

> **Can management increase firm value simply by choosing a different distribution policy?**

Firm value changes only if the policy changes:

- expected FCF;
- cost of capital;
- or investor perceptions of either.

Brigham defines:

### Target Distribution Ratio

Percentage of net income distributed through:

- cash dividends;
- plus stock repurchases.

### Target Payout Ratio

Percentage of net income paid specifically as **cash dividends**.

Therefore:

$$
\text{Payout Ratio}
\le
\text{Distribution Ratio}
$$

because repurchases are included only in the broader distribution ratio.

---

### 5.1 Dividend Irrelevance Theory

Miller and Modigliani argue that under idealized assumptions, firm value depends on:

- earning power of assets;
- business risk;

not on whether earnings are:

- paid as dividends;
- or retained.

#### Homemade Dividends

If company pays no dividend but investor wants cash, investor can sell part of holdings.

If company pays more dividend than investor wants, investor can reinvest the cash by buying more shares.

Therefore, **if transactions are costless and taxes irrelevant**, investors can manufacture their desired payout pattern themselves.

Under those assumptions:

> dividend policy is irrelevant to value.

### Critical Assumptions

The MM conclusion relies on ideal conditions such as:

- no taxes;
- no brokerage/transaction costs;
- no information asymmetry;
- rational capital markets.

Once these assumptions are relaxed, payout form may matter.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Dividend irrelevance does not mean dividends have no cash value.**
>
> It means that in a perfect market, changing the split between dividends and retained earnings does not by itself create value.

---

### 5.2 Dividend Preference / Bird-in-the-Hand Theory

Gordon and Lintner argue investors may prefer current dividends because dividends are perceived as more certain than future capital gains.

Intuition:

> “A bird in the hand is worth more than two in the bush.”

If investors perceive high dividends as reducing uncertainty, they may accept a lower required return on equity.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Higher perceived dividend certainty}
\Rightarrow
r_s\downarrow
\Rightarrow
P_0\uparrow
$$

### Agency-Cost Extension

Higher payouts can also reduce agency risk because:

1. less excess cash remains under managers’ control;
2. firms must return to capital markets more often, increasing external scrutiny.

Hence high payout may reduce opportunity for wasteful spending.

---

### 5.3 Tax-Effect Theory: Capital Gains Preferred

If dividends are taxed sooner or more heavily than capital gains, investors may prefer returns as capital appreciation.

Even if statutory rates are equal, capital gains have a timing advantage:

- dividend tax occurs when dividend is received;
- capital-gains tax can be deferred until shares are sold.

Deferral has value because:

$$
PV(\text{future tax})
<
\text{same tax paid today}
$$

Thus investors may require a higher pre-tax return from high-dividend stocks.

> [!CAUTION] Textbook Context
> Specific U.S. tax rates quoted in this edition are historical. Retain the economic principle—**tax timing and relative tax rates can affect payout preference**—rather than treating the statutory figures as current rules.

---

### 5.4 What Does the Evidence Say?

Brigham is cautious.

Testing payout theories is difficult because real firms differ simultaneously in:

- growth opportunities;
- risk;
- profitability;
- leverage;
- investor clientele;
- taxes;
- information.

Therefore empirical evidence does not produce a simple universal “high dividends always increase/decrease value” rule.

The chapter proceeds by examining additional real-world mechanisms: clientele, signaling, and stability.

---

## 6. 14.4 Clientele Effect

`[CF4 SUPPORTING CONTEXT]`

Different investors prefer different payout patterns.

### High-Dividend Clientele

May include investors needing regular current income, such as some:

- retirees;
- pension/endowment-type investors.

### Low-Dividend Clientele

Investors in accumulation phases may prefer:

- retention;
- capital gains;
- less current taxable cash income.

If a firm suddenly changes payout policy, existing shareholders whose preferences no longer match may sell.

Switching is not frictionless because of:

- brokerage costs;
- possible capital-gains taxes;
- search/reallocation costs.

Thus stable payout policy may have value because firms build a shareholder clientele consistent with that policy.

> [!IMPORTANT] Important Distinction
> **Clientele effect does not automatically prove one dividend policy is universally optimal.**
>
> It says different payout policies attract different investor groups.

## 7. 14.5 Information Content, or Signaling, Hypothesis

`[CORE CF4]`

### Information Asymmetry

MM's idealized framework assumes managers and investors have identical information.

In reality:

$$
\text{Management information}
>
\text{Public investor information}
$$

about future operating prospects.

Therefore payout announcements may convey information.

### Dividend Increase as Signal

Corporations are reluctant to raise regular dividends unless they believe the higher payout can be sustained.

Thus an unexpected increase can signal:

> management expects future earnings/cash flows to support the new dividend.

### Dividend Cut as Signal

Because firms strongly avoid dividend cuts, an unexpected cut is often interpreted as bad news about:

- earnings;
- cash flows;
- financial condition;
- sustainability of prior payout.

Hence stock prices frequently decline around dividend cuts.

### Important Interpretation

A stock-price rise after a dividend increase does **not** necessarily prove investors intrinsically prefer dividends.

The market may simply be updating expectations based on management's information.

Therefore:

```text
Dividend change
↓
possible information signal
↓
revised expected future cash flows / risk
↓
stock-price reaction
```

### Evidence

Brigham characterizes empirical evidence as mixed but concludes that dividend announcements clearly contain **some information content**.

Cuts tend to receive strongly negative reactions.

Increases can indicate that prior earnings improvement is expected to be permanent rather than temporary.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Price reaction to a dividend change ≠ proof of dividend preference.**
>
> The reaction may be driven by information about future cash flows.

## 8. 14.6 Implications for Dividend Stability

`[CORE CF4]`

Clientele and signaling combine to explain why firms value stable dividends.

### Reason 1 — Investor Consumption Needs

Some shareholders rely on dividend income.

Highly volatile dividends create inconvenience and uncertainty.

### Reason 2 — Avoid False Negative Signals

Suppose management cuts dividend only to finance an attractive investment.

Investors may instead interpret:

> “Management expects earnings to deteriorate.”

The price may fall even if the underlying reason was investment funding.

### Result: Dividend Smoothing

A value-maximizing firm therefore tends to avoid:

- sharp regular-dividend increases that cannot be maintained;
- dividend cuts except when necessary.

Because firms generally expect long-run earnings growth, a stable policy often means **slow, predictable dividend growth**, not literally a permanently constant nominal dividend.

### Practical Direction

Brigham anticipates the policy developed later:

> a **small sustainable regular dividend** supplemented by flexible stock repurchases when excess cash exists.

This preserves:

- reliability of regular dividends;
- flexibility of total shareholder distributions.

## 9. 14.7 Setting the Target Distribution Level: Residual Distribution Model

`[CF4 SUPPORTING CONTEXT]`

The residual model starts from two principles:

1. maximize shareholder value;
2. retain earnings only when the firm can reinvest them appropriately rather than distributing cash shareholders could invest themselves.

At the same time, retained earnings avoid the flotation costs and potential adverse signals associated with new equity issuance.

### Four-Step Residual Model

1. Determine optimal capital budget.
2. Determine equity required to finance that budget at target capital structure.
3. Use retained earnings to meet required equity.
4. Distribute only the earnings left over.

Formula:

$$
\boxed{
Distribution
=
Net\ Income
-
(Target\ Equity\ Ratio)(Capital\ Budget)
}
$$

subject to a minimum distribution of zero in the basic illustration.

### Textbook Example — T&W

Net income:

$$
\$60\text{ million}
$$

Target equity ratio:

$$
60\%
$$

#### Poor Opportunities

Capital budget:

$$
\$40
$$

Required equity:

$$
0.60(40)=24
$$

Distribution:

$$
60-24=36
$$

Distribution ratio:

$$
\frac{36}{60}=60\%
$$

#### Average Opportunities

Capital budget:

$$
70
$$

Required equity:

$$
42
$$

Distribution:

$$
18
$$

Ratio:

$$
30\%
$$

#### Good Opportunities

Capital budget:

$$
150
$$

Required equity:

$$
90
$$

Net income is only 60, so:

- retain all earnings;
- make no distribution;
- raise additional external equity if target structure must be maintained.

### Fundamental Relationship

$$
\text{Better investment opportunities}
\Rightarrow
\text{higher retained earnings need}
\Rightarrow
\text{lower residual distribution}
$$

This directly links investment policy and dividend policy.

---

## 10. 14.8 Residual Distribution Model in Practice

`[CF4 SUPPORTING CONTEXT]`

Strict year-by-year residual payout would make dividends highly volatile because:

- earnings fluctuate;
- capital budgets fluctuate.

But §14.6 showed that investors dislike unstable regular dividends.

Therefore Brigham says firms should use the residual model primarily to determine a **long-run target distribution/payout**, not mechanically set each year's dividend.

### Practical Process

1. Forecast earnings and investment opportunities over several years.
2. Determine average residual distributions under target capital structure.
3. Establish a sustainable target payout ratio.
4. Use flexible extras/repurchases for temporary excess cash.

### Low-Regular-Dividend-Plus-Extras

A company may:

- set a relatively low regular dividend;
- maintain it even in weak periods;
- add special dividends or repurchases when cash is unusually strong.

Investors then understand that the extra component is not permanent.

This reduces signaling damage if the extra distribution disappears later.

## 11. 14.9 A Tale of Two Cash Distributions: Dividends versus Stock Repurchases

`[CORE CF4]`

This section is the chapter's most important mechanical comparison.

Brigham uses **Benson Conglomerate**, a firm generating stable positive FCF, and asks how identical cash distributions differ if paid as:

- dividends;
- stock repurchases.

The analysis compares:

- financial statements;
- intrinsic value;
- number of shares;
- stock price;
- shareholder wealth.

---

### 11.1 Determining the Distribution

Benson has no debt in the example.

Projected 2011:

$$
Net\ Income=\$1{,}083.6
$$

Required investment in operating capital:

$$
\$412.0
$$

With a 100% target equity ratio:

$$
Required\ Equity=\$412.0
$$

Residual distribution:

$$
\$1{,}083.6-\$412.0
=
\boxed{\$671.6}
$$

The same residual appears as negative AFN/excess financing in the forecast.

### Free Cash Flow

For Benson:

$$
FCF
=
NOPAT
-
Investment\ in\ Operating\ Capital
$$

Projected 2011:

$$
FCF
=
\$1{,}083.6-\$412.0
=
\$671.6
$$

So the cash available for shareholder distribution is directly linked to free cash flow.

---

### 11.2 Distribution Form Does Not Change Value of Operations

Brigham estimates value of operations from expected FCF:

$$
V_{op}
=
PV(\text{future FCF})
$$

At the horizon:

$$
V_{op,T}
=
\frac{FCF_{T+1}}{WACC-g}
$$

The key conceptual result:

> **Whether residual cash is ultimately distributed through dividends or repurchases does not change operating FCF.**

Therefore, ignoring indirect effects such as taxes/signals:

$$
V_{operations}^{Dividend}
=
V_{operations}^{Repurchase}
$$

The operating business is the same.

---

### 11.3 Distributions as Dividends

Before dividend payment, firm holds cash/short-term investments.

When dividend is paid:

```text
Cash / financial assets ↓
Equity value ↓
Shares outstanding unchanged
```

Therefore stock price falls.

Ignoring taxes and unrelated news:

$$
\boxed{
P_{\text{after dividend}}
\approx
P_{\text{before dividend}}
-
DPS
}
$$

Benson illustration:

- price before distribution ≈ $10.75;
- DPS ≈ $0.67;
- ex-distribution price ≈ $10.07–$10.08.

### Why Shareholder Wealth Is Unchanged

Before:

$$
Wealth
=
Stock\ Value
$$

After:

$$
Wealth
=
Lower\ Stock\ Value
+
Cash\ Dividend
$$

Ignoring taxes:

$$
Total\ Wealth_{\text{before}}
=
Total\ Wealth_{\text{after}}
$$

The distribution changes **form of wealth**, not aggregate wealth.

---

### 11.4 Distributions as Stock Repurchases

Repurchase causes:

```text
Cash ↓
Total equity value ↓
Shares outstanding ↓
```

But in the textbook's frictionless intrinsic-value mechanics:

> **the repurchase itself does not change stock price per share.**

Why?

If everyone expected the post-repurchase price to be higher, current holders would refuse to sell at the lower pre-repurchase price.

If everyone expected it to be lower, holders would try to sell before the repurchase, pushing the price down immediately.

Arbitrage eliminates the discrete price jump caused purely by the repurchase transaction.

### Shares Repurchased

Let:

- $n_{Prior}$ = shares before repurchase;
- $n_{Post}$ = shares after;
- $P_{Prior}$ = price before;
- $Cash_{Rep}$ = cash distributed through repurchase.

Then:

$$
\boxed{
n_{Prior}-n_{Post}
=
\frac{Cash_{Rep}}{P_{Prior}}
}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
n_{Post}
=
n_{Prior}
-
\frac{Cash_{Rep}}{P_{Prior}}
}
$$

Benson:

$$
Cash_{Rep}=\$671.6
$$

$$
P_{Prior}=\$10.75
$$

$$
Shares\ Repurchased
=
\frac{671.6}{10.75}
\approx62.5
$$

Starting from 1,000 shares:

$$
n_{Post}
\approx937.5
$$

After repurchase, equity value falls by the cash paid out, but shares outstanding also fall proportionately, leaving per-share value essentially unchanged.

---

### 11.5 Three Core Results

Brigham summarizes the simplified dividend-versus-repurchase comparison:

#### Result 1

Ignoring taxes and signals:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Total market value of equity is the same under either distribution form}
}
$$

after appropriately accounting for cash distributed.

#### Result 2

A repurchase:

- reduces shares outstanding;
- does **not** itself change per-share stock price in the model.

#### Result 3

Over time, a repurchasing firm's per-share stock price grows faster than an otherwise identical dividend-paying firm's stock price because:

- fewer shares remain outstanding;
- dividend-paying stock experiences periodic price drops by DPS.

But:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Total shareholder return is the same}
}
$$

in the simplified no-tax/no-signal comparison.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Higher post-repurchase price path ≠ higher shareholder wealth by itself.**
>
> Dividend investors receive more cash distributions; repurchase investors retain fewer shares outstanding. Compare **total return**, not price alone.

## 12. 14.10 The Pros and Cons of Dividends and Repurchases

`[CORE CF4]`

### Advantages of Stock Repurchases

#### 1. Positive Signal

Repurchase announcement may signal that management considers shares undervalued.

#### 2. Shareholder Choice

With a repurchase:

- shareholders wanting cash can sell;
- those not wanting cash can retain shares.

With a dividend, all shareholders receive the cash distribution.

#### 3. Greater Flexibility

Regular dividends are **sticky**.

Management hesitates to raise them unless the increase is sustainable and strongly avoids cuts.

Therefore temporary excess cash is often better handled by repurchase than by permanently raising regular dividend.

#### 4. Supports Residual Distribution Policy

A firm can:

- maintain a stable, relatively conservative dividend;
- vary repurchases according to residual cash availability.

This allows total payout to respond to investment opportunities without making regular dividends volatile.

#### 5. Capital-Structure Adjustment

Debt-financed repurchases can quickly increase leverage and change capital structure.

#### 6. Employee Stock Options

Firms can repurchase shares and later deliver them to employees exercising options, reducing the need for new issuance and mitigating dilution.

---

### Disadvantages of Stock Repurchases

#### 1. Investors May Prefer Dependable Dividends

Some shareholders value stable cash income more than irregular repurchase opportunities.

#### 2. Information/Participation Concerns

Selling shareholders may not possess all information about company prospects.

Repurchase programs therefore need appropriate disclosure.

#### 3. Risk of Overpaying

Management may repurchase shares above intrinsic/equilibrium value.

If company overpays:

> wealth is transferred away from remaining shareholders.

This is one of the most important practical dangers of buybacks.

---

### Dividends vs Repurchases — Brigham's Practical Conclusion

#### Taxes and Choice

Repurchases can provide tax timing advantages because shareholders can choose whether to sell, while dividends force current cash receipt.

#### Stability

Regular dividend should be kept stable enough that it does not constrain operations or create avoidable negative signals.

#### Residual Cash

Flexible repurchases can absorb temporary or variable excess cash.

#### Special Situations

Repurchases are especially useful for:

- one-time cash events;
- major capital-structure changes;
- employee option programs.

### Practical Combined Policy

The chapter therefore points toward:

```text
Sustainable regular dividend
+
Flexible share repurchases
=
Stable investor expectations
+
Ability to distribute residual cash
```

rather than an all-dividend or all-repurchase extreme.

## 13. 14.11 Other Factors Influencing Distributions

`[CF4 SUPPORTING CONTEXT]`

Brigham groups additional influences into:

1. constraints on dividend payments;
2. availability/cost of alternative capital.

### Constraints

Examples include:

- bond-indenture restrictions;
- preferred dividend arrears;
- legal impairment-of-capital limits;
- availability of actual cash;
- tax rules designed to prevent inappropriate accumulation of earnings.

The durable lesson:

> **A firm's desired payout may be economically attractive but still constrained by contracts, law, liquidity, or financing conditions.**

### Alternative Sources of Capital

If new equity is expensive due to:

- flotation costs;
- negative issuance signals;

then retaining earnings becomes more attractive.

Likewise, if debt can be adjusted flexibly without sharply increasing risk/cost, firms have more room to stabilize dividends despite earnings fluctuations.

Control concerns can also discourage new share issuance and encourage retention.

## 14. 14.12 Summarizing the Distribution Policy Decision

`[CF4 SUPPORTING CONTEXT]`

A coherent payout policy must jointly consider:

```text
Investment opportunities
+
Target capital structure
+
Availability/cost of external financing
+
Taxes
+
Investor clientele
+
Signaling
+
Dividend stability
+
Liquidity and contractual constraints
```

No one payout ratio is optimal for every firm.

High-growth firms naturally retain more.

Mature cash-rich firms naturally distribute more.

The relevant question is not:

> “What payout ratio is generally best?”

but:

> “What payout policy best supports this firm's value-maximizing investment and financing strategy?”

## 15. 14.13 Stock Splits and Stock Dividends

`[BEYOND DIRECT CF4 SCOPE]`

### Stock Split

A stock split increases shares outstanding while proportionally reducing per-share values.

Example, 2-for-1:

```text
Shares × 2
EPS ÷ 2
DPS ÷ 2
Price per share ≈ ÷ 2
```

Ignoring signaling and market frictions, shareholder total wealth is unchanged.

### Stock Dividend

A stock dividend gives shareholders additional shares rather than cash.

Like a split, it increases number of shares and proportionally lowers per-share metrics.

### Why Use Them?

Brigham notes they may:

- move stock price into a preferred trading range;
- provide a signal of management confidence.

Observed positive price responses are likely due more to **signaling** than to the mechanical split itself.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Stock dividend ≠ cash dividend.**
>
> A stock dividend redistributes ownership units; it does not distribute operating cash to shareholders.

---

## 16. 14.14 Dividend Reinvestment Plans (DRIPs)

`[BEYOND DIRECT CF4 SCOPE]`

A DRIP allows shareholders to reinvest cash dividends automatically in company shares.

Two broad forms:

### Old-Stock DRIP

Dividend cash is used to buy existing shares in the secondary market.

Advantages:

- convenient;
- potentially lower transaction costs for small shareholders.

Does not itself raise new corporate equity capital.

### New-Stock DRIP

Dividend proceeds purchase newly issued shares from the company.

This can:

- raise new equity;
- reduce conventional flotation costs.

The existence of high DRIP participation can itself raise the question of whether shareholders truly need high cash dividends if many immediately reinvest them.

## 19. Important Distinctions

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **FCF ≠ net income**
>
> Distribution capacity depends on cash after required reinvestment, not accounting profit alone.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Distribution ratio ≠ dividend payout ratio**
>
> Distribution ratio includes dividends **and repurchases**.  
> Payout ratio refers to cash dividends.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Dividend preference theory ≠ signaling theory**
>
> Dividend preference says investors value dividends themselves more.  
> Signaling says dividend changes reveal management information.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Dividend cut ≠ automatically lower value only because less cash is paid**
>
> The cut can also alter investor expectations about future cash flows and risk.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Dividend stability ≠ always paying the same total distribution**
>
> Firms can keep regular dividends stable while varying repurchases.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Repurchase reduces total equity value ≠ repurchase reduces stock price**
>
> Total equity value falls because cash leaves the firm, but shares outstanding also fall; in the simplified intrinsic-value model, per-share price is unchanged by the repurchase itself.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Higher stock-price path under repurchases ≠ higher total return**
>
> Dividend shareholders receive cash that compensates for the price drop.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Residual model ≠ mechanically variable annual dividend**
>
> In practice, Brigham uses it to set long-run payout targets while smoothing regular dividends.

> [!WARNING] Important Distinction
> **Stock dividend ≠ shareholder cash distribution**
>
> It increases share count but does not return FCF to shareholders.

## 21. Chapter Synthesis

Jika Chapter 14 hanya boleh dipahami sebagai satu cerita, ceritanya adalah:

> **The firm's first obligation is not to maximize dividends; it is to invest in value-creating operations and finance them appropriately. Cash that remains after those needs is the shareholders' residual claim and should ultimately be returned in a form and pattern that supports shareholder value.**

High-growth companies often retain most cash because valuable investments consume capital.

Mature companies eventually reach a point where operating cash flow exceeds attractive reinvestment needs. At that point, refusing to distribute cash can create agency problems because management controls resources it may not be able to reinvest profitably.

How cash is returned then matters because real markets are not frictionless.

In an ideal MM world, investors can manufacture their own payout and distribution policy is irrelevant. In actual markets:

- taxes differ by form/timing;
- selling/reinvesting shares has costs;
- investors form clienteles;
- managers possess private information;
- dividend changes convey signals;
- excess cash can generate agency costs.

Because regular dividends are interpreted as a commitment, they become sticky. Firms therefore avoid setting regular dividends so high that normal fluctuations in earnings or investment opportunities would force a cut.

Repurchases solve part of this problem. They allow total distributions to vary without destabilizing the regular dividend. Shareholders needing cash can sell; others can retain shares. Repurchases can also serve recapitalization and employee-option needs.

Mechanically, a dividend and a repurchase are two ways of transferring the same cash out of the firm. Ignoring taxes and signaling:

```text
Dividend
→ cash ↓
→ equity value ↓
→ shares unchanged
→ price/share ↓ by roughly DPS

Repurchase
→ cash ↓
→ equity value ↓
→ shares outstanding ↓
→ price/share unchanged by transaction itself
```

Aggregate wealth is unchanged at the instant of either distribution in the frictionless model.

Therefore the chapter's final lesson is not “dividends are better” or “repurchases are better.”

It is:

> **choose a sustainable distribution policy consistent with investment opportunities, target financing, investor preferences, and information effects—typically combining stable regular dividends with flexible repurchases.**

## 25. Quick Reading Review

- Mature cash-rich firms tend to distribute more; high-growth firms retain more because reinvestment needs are larger.
- FCF—not net income alone—is the starting point for payout capacity.
- Brigham lists five uses of FCF: interest, principal, dividends, repurchases, and financial assets.
- Distribution policy concerns **amount, form, and stability** of shareholder payout.
- Dividend dates: declaration → ex-dividend → holder of record → payment.
- Ignoring other effects, stock price falls roughly by DPS when the stock goes ex-dividend.
- Stock repurchases can be open-market, tender-offer, or targeted.
- Distribution ratio includes both dividends and repurchases.
- Dividend payout ratio includes cash dividends only.
- MM dividend irrelevance holds under idealized perfect-market assumptions.
- Homemade dividends are central to MM's intuition.
- Bird-in-the-hand theory says investors may prefer more certain current dividends.
- Tax-effect theory says capital gains may be preferred because taxes can be lower or deferred.
- Clientele effect means different investors sort into firms with payout policies matching their needs.
- Dividend-policy changes can disrupt an existing clientele.
- Managers usually know more about future prospects than investors.
- Dividend increases/cuts therefore carry information content.
- Dividend cuts are especially powerful negative signals because firms try hard to avoid them.
- Signaling and clientele effects create a preference for stable regular dividends.
- Residual model: finance the optimal capital budget at target structure first, then distribute leftover earnings.
- Strict annual residual dividends would be too volatile; use residual logic for a **long-run target**.
- A low sustainable regular dividend can be supplemented with special dividends or repurchases.
- Dividend distribution reduces cash and equity value; shares outstanding stay constant.
- Repurchase reduces cash, equity value, and shares outstanding.
- In the simplified model, the repurchase itself does not change per-share stock price.
- Shareholder aggregate wealth is unchanged immediately after either distribution if taxes/signals are ignored.
- A repurchasing firm's stock price can rise faster over time because fewer shares remain—but total return need not exceed the dividend firm's.
- Repurchases offer flexibility and shareholder choice.
- Repurchases can be appropriate for temporary excess cash, recapitalizations, and employee options.
- A critical repurchase risk is **overpaying for the stock**.
- Brigham's practical direction is stable regular dividends plus flexible repurchases.
