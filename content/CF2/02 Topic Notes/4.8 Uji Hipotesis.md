# Section 1 — Big Picture

Hypothesis testing adalah decision procedure berbasis random sample.

General chain:

```text
population claim
      ↓
define H0 and H1
      ↓
choose test statistic
      ↓
derive null distribution
      ↓
choose α
      ↓
critical region / P-value
      ↓
decision
      ↓
interpret Type I / Type II / power
```

The test is not asking:

> “Apakah $H_0$ pasti benar?”

Tetapi:

> **“Apakah observed sample cukup extreme, jika $H_0$ benar, sehingga kita menolak $H_0$ pada significance level yang ditetapkan?”**

---

# Section 2 — Statistical Hypothesis

Miller mendefinisikan **statistical hypothesis** sebagai assertion/conjecture tentang distribution dari satu atau lebih random variables.

Hypothesis dapat menyangkut:

- parameter value;
- hubungan antarparameter;
- distribution form.

## 2.1 Simple vs Composite

**Simple hypothesis:** completely specifies relevant distribution.

Example:

$$
H:\theta=0.90.
$$

**Composite hypothesis:** lebih dari satu parameter value masih possible.

Example:

$$
H:\mu>100.
$$

Mental distinction:

$$
\boxed{
\text{simple}
=
\text{one fully specified parameter configuration}
}
$$

versus

$$
\boxed{
\text{composite}
=
\text{a set/range of parameter values}
}.
$$

---

# Section 3 — Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Notation:

$$
\boxed{
H_0
\quad\text{vs}\quad
H_1
}.
$$

Miller biasanya menaruh equality di null karena null distribution dari test statistic perlu ditentukan secara spesifik.

Examples:

### Two-sided

$$
H_0:\mu=\mu_0
$$

versus

$$
H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0.
$$

### Right-sided

$$
H_0:\mu=\mu_0
$$

versus

$$
H_1:\mu>\mu_0.
$$

### Left-sided

$$
H_0:\mu=\mu_0
$$

versus

$$
H_1:\mu<\mu_0.
$$

> [!DANGER] Hypothesis-Direction Trap
> **Tail direction comes from $H_1$.**
>
> - $H_1: \theta>\theta_0$ → right tail.
> - $H_1: \theta<\theta_0$ → left tail.
> - $H_1: \theta\ne\theta_0$ → two tails.

---

# Section 4 — Test Statistic, Critical Region, and Decision

A **test statistic** is a statistic computed from the sample and used to decide whether to reject $H_0$.

Possible values are divided into:

- non-rejection/acceptance region;
- **critical region** = rejection region.

If observed test statistic lies in critical region:

$$
\boxed{
\text{reject }H_0.
}
$$

Otherwise:

$$
\boxed{
\text{do not reject }H_0.
}
$$

Miller sometimes uses traditional “accept $H_0$” language, but the safer interpretation is:

> failure to reject is not proof that $H_0$ is true.

---

# Section 5 — Type I and Type II Errors

Two errors are possible because sample is random.

## 5.1 Type I Error

Reject $H_0$ when $H_0$ is true.

$$
\boxed{
\alpha
=
P(
\text{reject }H_0
\mid
H_0\text{ true}
)
}.
$$

$\alpha$ is also significance level / test size for simple null.

## 5.2 Type II Error

Fail to reject / accept $H_0$ when alternative is true.

For simple alternative:

$$
\boxed{
\beta
=
P(
\text{do not reject }H_0
\mid
H_1\text{ true}
)
}.
$$

## 5.3 Decision Table

| Decision | $H_0$ true | $H_1$ true |
|---|---|---|
| Do not reject $H_0$ | Correct | Type II error |
| Reject $H_0$ | Type I error | Correct |

> [!TIP] Memory Aid
> **Type I = false positive rejection.**  
> **Type II = missed alternative.**

---

# Section 6 — $\alpha$–$\beta$ Trade-Off

Miller's Binomial medication example shows that, for fixed $n$, moving the critical boundary changes both errors.

Example:

$$
H_0:\theta=0.90
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\theta=0.60.
$$

If reject when

$$
X\le14,
$$

Miller gives:

$$
\alpha=0.0114,
\qquad
\beta=0.1255.
$$

If boundary is shifted to reject when

$$
X\le15,
$$

then:

$$
\alpha=0.0433,
\qquad
\beta=0.0509.
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{smaller }\beta
\text{ came with larger }\alpha.
}
$$

For fixed sample size, reducing one error generally makes the other worse.

Miller notes a direct way to make both smaller:

$$
\boxed{
\text{increase }n.
}
$$

---

# Section 7 — Power

Hogg, McKean & Craig defines power function:

$$
\boxed{
\gamma(\theta)
=
P_\theta(
\text{reject }H_0
)
}.
$$

Under the alternative, this is probability of correctly rejecting $H_0$.

For a simple alternative:

$$
\boxed{
\text{power}
=
1-\beta.
}
$$

### Size vs Power

Same rejection event; different parameter region:

$$
\alpha
=
P_{\theta_0}(\text{reject }H_0)
$$

for simple null, while

$$
\gamma(\theta)
=
P_\theta(\text{reject }H_0)
$$

for alternative values.

A useful test aims for:

- small rejection probability under $H_0$;
- high rejection probability under $H_1$.

> [!IMPORTANT] Power Is Parameter-Dependent
> For composite alternatives, power is generally a **function of $\theta$**, not a single number.

---

# Section 8 — Constructing a Critical Value from $\alpha$

Miller's Normal example:

$$
X_i\sim N(\mu,1).
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\mu=\mu_0
$$

versus

$$
H_1:\mu>\mu_0.
$$

Use critical region:

$$
\bar X>K.
$$

Under $H_0$:

$$
\bar X
\sim
N\left(
\mu_0,\frac1n
\right).
$$

For significance level $\alpha$:

$$
P_{\mu_0}(\bar X>K)=\alpha.
$$

Standardize:

$$
\frac{K-\mu_0}{1/\sqrt n}
=
z_\alpha.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
K
=
\mu_0
+
\frac{z_\alpha}{\sqrt n}.
}
$$

General mental model:

```text
set α
  ↓
null distribution
  ↓
critical quantile
  ↓
critical region
```

---

# Section 9 — P-Value

Miller defines P-value as a measure of how extreme observed evidence is under $H_0$, using tail direction implied by $H_1$.

Decision rule:

$$
\boxed{
\text{reject }H_0
\quad\text{if}\quad
\text{P-value}\le\alpha.
}
$$

Otherwise:

$$
\boxed{
\text{do not reject }H_0.
}
$$

### One-Sided Normal

Right-tail:

$$
\boxed{
p
=
P(Z\ge z_{\text{obs}})
}.
$$

Left-tail:

$$
\boxed{
p
=
P(Z\le z_{\text{obs}})
}.
$$

### Two-Sided Symmetric Normal

$$
\boxed{
p
=
2P(
Z\ge|z_{\text{obs}}|
)
}.
$$

> [!DANGER] P-Value Interpretation Trap
> P-value is **not**:
>
> $$
> P(H_0\text{ true}\mid\text{data}).
> $$
>
> It is a probability of data at least as extreme, computed under the null model.

---

# Section 10 — Critical-Value Method vs P-Value Method

These methods lead to the same reject/do-not-reject decision if applied consistently.

### Critical Value Method

1. choose $\alpha$;
2. determine critical region;
3. compute test statistic;
4. compare statistic with critical value.

### P-Value Method

1. compute test statistic;
2. compute P-value;
3. compare P-value with $\alpha$.

Equivalent decision:

$$
\boxed{
\text{statistic in critical region}
\iff
p\le\alpha.
}
$$

# Section 11 — One-Sample Mean Tests

## 11.1 Known $\sigma$: Exact $Z$ Test

Assume:

$$
X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),
$$

with $\sigma$ known.

Test:

$$
H_0:\mu=\mu_0.
$$

Statistic:

$$
\boxed{
Z
=
\frac{
\bar X-\mu_0
}{
\sigma/\sqrt n
}.
}
$$

Under $H_0$:

$$
Z\sim N(0,1).
$$

### Rejection Rules

Two-sided:

$$
H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\boxed{
|Z|\ge z_{\alpha/2}
}.
$$

Right-sided:

$$
H_1:\mu>\mu_0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\boxed{
Z\ge z_\alpha
}.
$$

Left-sided:

$$
H_1:\mu<\mu_0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\boxed{
Z\le-z_\alpha
}.
$$

---

## 11.2 Textbook Example — Cookie Weight

Given:

$$
\sigma=0.16,
\quad
n=25,
\quad
\bar x=8.091.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\mu=8
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\mu\ne8
$$

at

$$
\alpha=0.01.
$$

Statistic:

$$
z
=
\frac{
8.091-8
}{
0.16/\sqrt{25}
}
=
2.84.
$$

Critical value:

$$
z_{0.005}=2.575.
$$

Since:

$$
2.84>2.575,
$$

reject $H_0$.

Miller gives:

$$
\boxed{
p=0.0046.
}
$$

Since

$$
0.0046<0.01,
$$

same decision.

# Section 12 — Large-Sample Mean Test, $\sigma$ Unknown

For sufficiently large $n$, Miller uses CLT and substitutes $S$ for $\sigma$:

$$
\boxed{
Z
=
\frac{
\bar X-\mu_0
}{
S/\sqrt n
}.
}
$$

`[TEXTBOOK CONTEXT]`

Miller uses:

$$
n\ge30
$$

as a practical large-sample rule.

This is an approximation unless exact Normal known-$\sigma$ conditions apply.

### Textbook Tire Example

$$
n=100,
\quad
\bar x=21{,}819,
\quad
s=1{,}295.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\mu=22{,}000
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\mu<22{,}000.
$$

Statistic:

$$
z
=
\frac{
21{,}819-22{,}000
}{
1{,}295/\sqrt{100}
}
=
-1.40.
$$

For

$$
\alpha=0.05,
$$

critical value:

$$
-1.645.
$$

Since

$$
-1.40>-1.645,
$$

do not reject.

Miller gives:

$$
p=0.0808.
$$

# Section 13 — One-Sample $t$ Test

If:

- sample small;
- $\sigma$ unknown;
- population Normal;

then:

$$
\boxed{
T
=
\frac{
\bar X-\mu_0
}{
S/\sqrt n
}
\sim
t_{n-1}
}
$$

under $H_0$.

### Degrees of Freedom

$$
\boxed{
\nu=n-1.
}
$$

### Rejection Rules

Two-sided:

$$
|T|
\ge
t_{\alpha/2,n-1}.
$$

Right-sided:

$$
T
\ge
t_{\alpha,n-1}.
$$

Left-sided:

$$
T
\le
-t_{\alpha,n-1}.
$$

> [!DANGER] $Z$ vs $t$ Trap
> Small Normal sample + unknown $\sigma$ → $t$, not $Z$ with $s$ silently substituted.

---

# Section 14 — Tests for Difference of Two Means

Target:

$$
\mu_1-\mu_2.
$$

General null:

$$
\boxed{
H_0:
\mu_1-\mu_2=\delta.
}
$$

## 14.1 Known Variances

Independent Normal samples:

$$
\boxed{
Z
=
\frac{
\bar X_1-\bar X_2-\delta
}{
\sqrt{
\sigma_1^2/n_1
+
\sigma_2^2/n_2
}
}.
}
$$

Use Normal tail rule according to $H_1$.

## 14.2 Large Samples, Unknown Variances

Approximate statistic:

$$
\boxed{
Z
=
\frac{
\bar X_1-\bar X_2-\delta
}{
\sqrt{
S_1^2/n_1
+
S_2^2/n_2
}
}.
}
$$

> [!DANGER] Variance-of-Difference Trap
> Because samples are independent:
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_1-\bar X_2)
> =
> \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_1)
> +
> \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_2).
> $$

---

# Section 15 — Pooled Two-Sample $t$ Test

For small independent samples from Normal populations with common unknown variance:

$$
\sigma_1^2
=
\sigma_2^2
=
\sigma^2.
$$

Pooled variance:

$$
\boxed{
S_p^2
=
\frac{
(n_1-1)S_1^2
+
(n_2-1)S_2^2
}{
n_1+n_2-2
}.
}
$$

Statistic:

$$
\boxed{
T
=
\frac{
\bar X_1-\bar X_2-\delta
}{
S_p
\sqrt{
1/n_1+1/n_2
}
}
\sim
t_{n_1+n_2-2}.
}
$$

Degrees of freedom:

$$
\boxed{
\nu=n_1+n_2-2.
}
$$

### Assumptions

1. independent samples;
2. Normal populations;
3. equal population variances.

> [!DANGER] Pooling Trap
> Never pool merely because two sample variances happen to look similar. Equal population variance is a model assumption.

---

## 15.1 Textbook Paint Example

$$
n_1=n_2=4,
$$

$$
\bar x_1=546,
\quad
s_1=31,
$$

$$
\bar x_2=492,
\quad
s_2=26.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\mu_1-\mu_2>0.
$$

Miller gets:

$$
s_p=28.609,
$$

$$
t=2.67.
$$

Critical:

$$
t_{0.05,6}=1.943.
$$

Thus:

$$
2.67>1.943
$$

and reject $H_0$.

P-value:

$$
\boxed{
0.0185.
}
$$

# Section 16 — Paired Samples

Paired / before-after data are **not independent two-sample observations**.

Define differences:

$$
D_i=X_i-Y_i.
$$

Then test mean difference using one-sample methodology:

$$
\boxed{
H_0:\mu_D=\delta.
}
$$

For small Normal difference sample:

$$
\boxed{
T
=
\frac{
\bar D-\delta
}{
S_D/\sqrt n
}.
}
$$

> [!IMPORTANT] Paired-vs-Independent Trap
> Paired design becomes:
>
> $$
> \text{one sample of differences},
> $$
>
> not a two-independent-sample test.

---

# Section 17 — One-Population Variance Test

For random sample from a **Normal population**:

$$
\frac{
(n-1)S^2
}{
\sigma^2
}
\sim
\chi^2_{n-1}.
$$

Testing:

$$
H_0:
\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2
$$

uses:

$$
\boxed{
\chi^2
=
\frac{
(n-1)S^2
}{
\sigma_0^2
}.
}
$$

### Right-Tailed

$$
H_1:\sigma^2>\sigma_0^2
$$

reject for sufficiently large $\chi^2$.

### Left-Tailed

$$
H_1:\sigma^2<\sigma_0^2
$$

reject for sufficiently small $\chi^2$.

### Two-Sided

$$
H_1:\sigma^2\ne\sigma_0^2
$$

split $\alpha/2$ into both tails.

> [!ASSUMPTION]
> Exact chi-square variance test requires Normal population.

---

## 17.1 Textbook Semiconductor Example

$$
n=18,
\qquad
s^2=0.68.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\sigma^2=0.36
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\sigma^2>0.36.
$$

At:

$$
\alpha=0.05,
$$

test statistic:

$$
\chi^2
=
\frac{
17(0.68)
}{
0.36
}
=
32.11.
$$

Critical value:

$$
\chi^2_{0.05,17}
=
27.587.
$$

Since:

$$
32.11>27.587,
$$

reject $H_0$.

---

# Section 18 — Ratio of Two Variances: $F$ Test

For two independent samples from Normal populations, test:

$$
H_0:
\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2.
$$

Under $H_0$:

$$
\boxed{
F
=
\frac{
S_1^2
}{
S_2^2
}
\sim
F_{n_1-1,n_2-1}
}
$$

with appropriate numerator orientation.

For right-sided alternative:

$$
H_1:
\sigma_1^2>\sigma_2^2,
$$

use:

$$
F
=
\frac{S_1^2}{S_2^2}
$$

and reject for large values.

For two-sided test, Miller places the **larger observed sample variance in numerator** and uses the corresponding right-tail critical value.

### Reciprocal Rule

$$
F\sim F_{\nu_1,\nu_2}
$$

implies

$$
\boxed{
\frac1F
\sim
F_{\nu_2,\nu_1}.
}
$$

> [!DANGER] F Orientation Trap
> Swapping numerator and denominator also swaps the degrees of freedom.

---

## 18.1 Textbook Steel Example

$$
n_1=13,
\quad
s_1^2=19.2,
$$

$$
n_2=16,
\quad
s_2^2=3.5.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\sigma_1^2\ne\sigma_2^2
$$

at:

$$
\alpha=0.02.
$$

Since first variance is larger:

$$
F
=
\frac{19.2}{3.5}
=
5.49.
$$

Critical:

$$
f_{0.01,12,15}
=
3.67.
$$

Therefore:

$$
5.49>3.67
$$

and reject equal variances.

# Section 19 — One-Proportion Test

Let:

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,\theta).
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\theta=\theta_0.
$$

## 19.1 Exact Binomial Logic

For an upper alternative:

$$
H_1:\theta>\theta_0,
$$

reject for sufficiently large $X$.

For lower alternative:

$$
H_1:\theta<\theta_0,
$$

reject for sufficiently small $X$.

Exact critical cutoff is chosen using Binomial tail probabilities under $\theta_0$.

## 19.2 Large-Sample Normal Approximation

Under $H_0$:

$$
E[X]=n\theta_0,
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)
=
n\theta_0(1-\theta_0).
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Z
=
\frac{
X-n\theta_0
}{
\sqrt{
n\theta_0(1-\theta_0)
}
}
}.
$$

Equivalent sample-proportion form:

$$
\boxed{
Z
=
\frac{
\hat\theta-\theta_0
}{
\sqrt{
\theta_0(1-\theta_0)/n
}
}.
}
$$

> [!DANGER] Null-SE Trap
> For a hypothesis test, standard error is built using the **null parameter $\theta_0$**, not $\hat\theta$.
>
> This differs from Miller's approximate confidence interval for a proportion, where the SE uses $\hat\theta$.

---

# Section 20 — Difference of Two Proportions

For two independent large Binomial samples, a common test is:

$$
H_0:
\theta_1=\theta_2.
$$

Under $H_0$, both groups share a common proportion.

Use pooled estimator:

$$
\boxed{
\hat\theta
=
\frac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2}.
}
$$

Then:

$$
\boxed{
Z
=
\frac{
\hat\theta_1-\hat\theta_2
}{
\sqrt{
\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)
\left(
1/n_1+1/n_2
\right)
}
}.
}
$$

> [!DANGER] CI-vs-Test SE Trap
> For confidence interval of $\theta_1-\theta_2$, Miller uses separate sample proportions in the standard error.
>
> For testing equality:
>
> $$
> H_0:\theta_1=\theta_2,
> $$
>
> the null imposes a common proportion, so the test uses a **pooled proportion**.

---

# Section 21 — Differences Among $k$ Proportions

Miller §13.6 extends proportion testing to:

$$
k
$$

independent Binomial populations.

Null:

$$
\boxed{
H_0:
\theta_1=\theta_2=\cdots=\theta_k.
}
$$

Pooled proportion:

$$
\boxed{
\hat\theta
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^k x_i
}{
\sum_{i=1}^k n_i
}.
}
$$

Miller gives test statistic:

$$
\boxed{
\chi^2
=
\sum_{i=1}^{k}
\frac{
(x_i-n_i\hat\theta)^2
}{
n_i\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)
}
\sim
\chi^2_{k-1}
}
$$

approximately under $H_0$.

Degrees of freedom:

$$
\boxed{
\nu=k-1.
}
$$

# Section 22 — Hypothesis Testing and Confidence Intervals

Two-sided tests and confidence intervals are closely related.

For a parameter $\theta$, a two-sided level-$\alpha$ test of:

$$
H_0:\theta=\theta_0
$$

usually rejects exactly when the corresponding:

$$
(1-\alpha)100\%
$$

confidence interval excludes $\theta_0$.

Examples:

### Difference

If CI for

$$
\mu_1-\mu_2
$$

does not contain:

$$
0,
$$

the corresponding equality null is rejected.

### Ratio

If CI for:

$$
\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_2^2}
$$

does not contain:

$$
1,
$$

equal variances are rejected.

> [!TIP] Fast Cross-Check
> Difference benchmark:
>
> $$
> 0.
> $$
>
> Ratio benchmark:
>
> $$
> 1.
> $$

# Section 23 — UMP / MLR Connection from Hogg §8.2

`[CORE CF2 — Hogg §8.2]`

Hogg, McKean & Craig develops one-sided **uniformly most powerful (UMP)** tests under monotone likelihood ratio structure.

Suppose:

$$
H_0:\theta\le\theta_0
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\theta>\theta_0.
$$

If likelihood has the appropriate monotone likelihood ratio in statistic:

$$
Y=u(X_1,\ldots,X_n),
$$

the UMP level-$\alpha$ test has an upper-tail form:

$$
\boxed{
\text{reject }H_0
\text{ if }
Y\ge c.
}
$$

Critical value:

$$
\boxed{
P_{\theta_0}(Y\ge c)
=
\alpha.
}
$$

Reverse one-sided case:

$$
H_0:\theta\ge\theta_0
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\theta<\theta_0
$$

leads to lower-tail rule:

$$
\boxed{
Y\le c.
}
$$

### Why This Matters for CF2

The conceptual message is:

> A one-sided critical region is not an arbitrary convention. It reflects how likelihood evidence moves monotonically as the parameter moves into the alternative.

Power function:

$$
\gamma(\theta)
=
P_\theta(Y\in C)
$$

typically moves upward into the alternative under the MLR conditions.

> [!NOTE]
> Detailed proof of MLR $\Rightarrow$ UMP is not necessary for routine Miller Chapter 13 calculations, but Hogg §8.2 is explicitly mapped by the syllabus and supports the deeper logic of test direction and power.

# Section 24 — Loss and Risk

Miller §12.3 connects testing with decision theory.

Actions:

- $a_0$: choose $H_0$;
- $a_1$: choose $H_1$.

Under true $\theta_0$:

$$
\boxed{
R(d,\theta_0)
=
L(a_0,\theta_0)
+
\alpha(d)
[
L(a_1,\theta_0)-L(a_0,\theta_0)
].
}
$$

Under true $\theta_1$:

$$
\boxed{
R(d,\theta_1)
=
L(a_1,\theta_1)
+
\beta(d)
[
L(a_0,\theta_1)-L(a_1,\theta_1)
].
}
$$

The positive loss differences show why both Type I and Type II errors matter.

This is supporting conceptual material, not the main calculation engine for standard $Z/t/\chi^2/F$ tests.

# Section 25 — Universal Exam Workflow

Use this every time:

### Step 1 — Identify Parameter

Is the target:

- mean?
- difference of means?
- variance?
- ratio of variances?
- proportion?
- difference/equality of proportions?

### Step 2 — Write $H_0$ and $H_1$

Direction matters.

### Step 3 — Determine Tail

From $H_1$:

- $>$ → right;
- $<$ → left;
- $\ne$ → two-sided.

### Step 4 — State Assumptions

Examples:

- $\sigma$ known?
- Normal population?
- large $n$?
- independent samples?
- paired?
- equal variances?

### Step 5 — Choose Statistic

$$
Z,\quad
t,\quad
\chi^2,\quad
F.
$$

### Step 6 — Compute Under $H_0$

Substitute **null parameter value**.

### Step 7 — Critical Region or P-Value

Use correct tail and df.

### Step 8 — Decision

$$
\text{reject}
$$

or

$$
\text{do not reject}.
$$

### Step 9 — Interpret

Phrase conclusion relative to $H_1$, not as proof of $H_0$.

|---|---|---|

| One mean | large sample, $\sigma$ unknown | approximate $Z=(\bar X-\mu_0)/(S/\sqrt n)$ |
| One mean | small Normal, $\sigma$ unknown | $T=(\bar X-\mu_0)/(S/\sqrt n)$ |
| Difference means | independent, variances known | $Z$ |

| Ratio variances | independent Normal samples | $F=S_1^2/S_2^2$ |

# Section 27 — High-Yield Exam Traps

## 27.1 Tail Direction Trap

Do not determine tail from sign of sample statistic.

Tail comes from:

$$
H_1.
$$

---

## 27.2 Null-Parameter Trap

Test statistic uses the null value:

$$
\mu_0,
\quad
\sigma_0^2,
\quad
\theta_0.
$$

---

## 27.3 P-Value Trap

P-value is computed under $H_0$, not under estimated alternative.

---

## 27.4 “Accept $H_0$” Trap

A non-significant result does not prove null truth.

Safer wording:

$$
\boxed{
\text{do not reject }H_0.
}
$$

---

## 27.5 $Z$ vs $t$ Trap

- $\sigma$ known → $Z$.
- small Normal sample + unknown $\sigma$ → $t$.
- large sample + unknown $\sigma$ → Miller's approximate $Z$ framework.

---

## 27.6 Degrees-of-Freedom Trap

One-sample $t$:

$$
n-1.
$$

Pooled two-sample $t$:

$$
n_1+n_2-2.
$$

Variance $\chi^2$:

$$
n-1.
$$

Variance ratio:

$$
(n_1-1,n_2-1).
$$

$k$ proportions:

$$
k-1.
$$

---

## 27.7 Independent vs Paired Trap

Matched observations:

$$
D_i=X_i-Y_i.
$$

Use one-sample test on differences.

---

## 27.8 Pooling Variances Trap

Pooled $t$ requires equal population variances.

---

## 27.9 CI SE vs Test SE for Proportions

CI for one proportion often uses:

$$
\hat p
$$

inside SE.

Test of:

$$
H_0:p=p_0
$$

uses:

$$
p_0.
$$

Two-proportion equality test uses pooled:

$$
\hat p
=
\frac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2}.
$$

---

## 27.10 Variance Test Normality Trap

Exact $\chi^2$ and $F$ variance tests require Normal populations.

---

## 27.11 $F$ Orientation Trap

If numerator changes, df order changes.

---

## 27.12 $\alpha$ Chosen After Data Trap

Miller explicitly warns against adjusting $\alpha$ after seeing the result to force significance.

---

## 27.13 Type I vs Type II Trap

Type I:

$$
H_0\text{ true but rejected}.
$$

Type II:

$$
H_1\text{ true but }H_0\text{ not rejected}.
$$

---

## 27.14 Power Trap

Power is:

$$
1-\beta
$$

only at a specified simple alternative; for composite alternatives think:

$$
\gamma(\theta).
$$

---

# Section 28 — Worked Exam-Style Cases

## Case A — Right-Tailed One-Mean $Z$ Test

Suppose:

$$
n=64,
\quad
\bar x=103,
\quad
\sigma=8.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\mu=100
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\mu>100
$$

at:

$$
\alpha=0.05.
$$

Statistic:

$$
z
=
\frac{
103-100
}{
8/\sqrt{64}
}
=
3.
$$

Critical value:

$$
z_{0.05}=1.645.
$$

Since:

$$
3>1.645,
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{reject }H_0.
}
$$

---

## Case B — Two-Sided One-Mean $t$ Test

Normal sample:

$$
n=16,
\quad
\bar x=51,
\quad
s=4.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\mu=50
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\mu\ne50.
$$

Statistic:

$$
t
=
\frac{
51-50
}{
4/\sqrt{16}
}
=
1.
$$

Degrees of freedom:

$$
15.
$$

Decision is made by comparing:

$$
|1|
$$

with:

$$
t_{\alpha/2,15}.
$$

---

## Case C — One Variance

Normal sample:

$$
n=20,
\quad
s^2=25.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:\sigma^2=16
$$

vs

$$
H_1:\sigma^2>16.
$$

Statistic:

$$
\chi^2
=
\frac{
19(25)
}{
16
}
=
29.6875.
$$

Compare with:

$$
\chi^2_{\alpha,19}.
$$

---

## Case D — One Proportion

Suppose:

$$
n=400,
\quad
x=232.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:p=0.50
$$

vs

$$
H_1:p>0.50.
$$

Sample proportion:

$$
\hat p
=
0.58.
$$

Null SE:

$$
\sqrt{
\frac{
0.5(0.5)
}{
400
}
}
=
0.025.
$$

Statistic:

$$
z
=
\frac{
0.58-0.50
}{
0.025
}
=
3.2.
$$

This is a right-tail test.

---

## Case E — Two Proportions

Group 1:

$$
x_1=120,
\quad
n_1=200.
$$

Group 2:

$$
x_2=80,
\quad
n_2=160.
$$

Test:

$$
H_0:p_1=p_2.
$$

Sample proportions:

$$
\hat p_1=0.60,
$$

$$
\hat p_2=0.50.
$$

Pooled null estimate:

$$
\hat p
=
\frac{
120+80
}{
200+160
}
=
\frac{200}{360}.
$$

Statistic:

$$
Z
=
\frac{
0.60-0.50
}{
\sqrt{
\hat p(1-\hat p)
\left(
1/200+1/160
\right)
}
}.
$$

The pooled proportion is essential because equality is assumed under $H_0$.

# Section 29 — Formula Map

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Type I error | $\alpha=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ true})$ |
| Type II error | $\beta=P(\text{do not reject }H_0\mid H_1\text{ true})$ |
| Power function | $\gamma(\theta)=P_\theta(\text{reject }H_0)$ |
| Simple-alt power | $1-\beta$ |
| P-value rule | reject if $p\le\alpha$ |
| One-mean $Z$ | $(\bar X-\mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)$ |
| Large-sample mean $Z$ | $(\bar X-\mu_0)/(S/\sqrt n)$ |
| One-mean $t$ | $(\bar X-\mu_0)/(S/\sqrt n)$, df $n-1$ |
| Difference means $Z$ | $(\bar X_1-\bar X_2-\delta)/\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1+\sigma_2^2/n_2}$ |
| Large-sample difference means | replace $\sigma_i$ by $S_i$ |
| Pooled variance | $S_p^2=\frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2+(n_2-1)S_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}$ |
| Pooled $t$ | $(\bar X_1-\bar X_2-\delta)/[S_p\sqrt{1/n_1+1/n_2}]$ |
| One variance | $\chi^2=(n-1)S^2/\sigma_0^2$ |
| Ratio variances | $F=S_1^2/S_2^2$ |
| One proportion | $Z=(\hat p-p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}$ |
| Two proportions pooled | $\hat p=(x_1+x_2)/(n_1+n_2)$ |
| Two-proportion $Z$ | $(\hat p_1-\hat p_2)/\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)(1/n_1+1/n_2)}$ |
| $k$ proportions pooled | $\hat p=\sum x_i/\sum n_i$ |
| $k$ proportions test | $\chi^2=\sum\frac{(x_i-n_i\hat p)^2}{n_i\hat p(1-\hat p)}$ |
| UMP upper-tail form | reject if $Y\ge c$, $P_{\theta_0}(Y\ge c)=\alpha$ |

`[TEXTBOOK-BASED EXPECTATION]` — pattern diturunkan dari silabus dan source mechanics, bukan klaim frekuensi past exam.

| Narasi Soal | Test | Statistic / Structure | Trap | Shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| “P-value” | evidence measure | correct tail probability under $H_0$ | probability null true | observed-extremeness under null |
| “two-sided test vs CI” | duality | null excluded from CI | wrong benchmark | difference→0, ratio→1 |
