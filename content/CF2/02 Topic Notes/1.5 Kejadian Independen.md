# Section 1 — Intuisi: Informasi Satu Event Tidak Mengubah Probabilitas Event Lain

Dari [[1.4 Probabilitas Bersyarat]], kita tahu bahwa mengetahui event $A$ terjadi biasanya mengubah probability event $B$:

$$
P(B\mid A)
\neq
P(B).
$$

Tetapi pada pasangan event tertentu, informasi bahwa $A$ telah terjadi **tidak mengubah** probability $B$.

Dalam kondisi tersebut:

$$
P(B\mid A)=P(B).
$$

Demikian juga, jika $P(B)>0$,

$$
P(A\mid B)=P(A).
$$

Inilah intuition dasar **independence**:

> knowing one event occurred provides no probability update about the other event.

Mental model:

```text
Before knowing A:
P(B)

After knowing A:
P(B | A)

If unchanged:
P(B | A) = P(B)
        ↓
A and B independent
```

Hogg memotivasi konsep ini dengan two-coin-flip experiment. Event “head pada toss pertama” tidak mengubah probability “tail pada toss kedua”, sehingga kedua event tersebut independent.

---

# Section 2 — Definisi Formal Independence

Untuk dua events $A$ dan $B$:

$$
\boxed{
A \text{ dan } B \text{ independent}
\iff
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
}
$$

Jika equality tidak berlaku:

$$
\boxed{
P(A\cap B)\ne P(A)P(B)
}
$$

maka $A$ dan $B$ **dependent**.

> [!IMPORTANT] Why product form is the safest definition
> Definisi product:
>
> $$
> P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
> $$
>
> tetap dapat digunakan bahkan ketika $P(A)=0$ atau $P(B)=0$, sedangkan conditional expressions membutuhkan denominator positif.

---

# Section 3 — Hubungan dengan Conditional Probability

Jika $P(A)>0$, maka

$$
P(B\mid A)
=
\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}.
$$

Jika $A$ dan $B$ independent,

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B),
$$

jadi

$$
P(B\mid A)
=
\frac{P(A)P(B)}{P(A)}
=
P(B).
$$

Dengan cara yang sama, jika $P(B)>0$:

$$
P(A\mid B)=P(A).
$$

Jadi, untuk positive-probability events:

$$
\boxed{
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
}
$$

equivalent dengan

$$
\boxed{
P(B\mid A)=P(B)
}
$$

dan

$$
\boxed{
P(A\mid B)=P(A).
}
$$

### Three equivalent checks

Jika denominators valid:

1. Product criterion:
   $$
   P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
   $$

2. Conditional criterion:
   $$
   P(B\mid A)=P(B)
   $$

3. Reverse conditional criterion:
   $$
   P(A\mid B)=P(A)
   $$

> [!TIP] Exam Shortcut
> Pilih criterion yang menggunakan data yang **sudah diberikan** oleh soal. Jangan menghitung conditional probability jika product test sudah langsung tersedia.

---

# Section 4 — Hogg Example: Coin Toss

Flip fair coin twice:

$$
S=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}.
$$

Define:

$$
A=\{\text{head pada first flip}\}=\{HH,HT\},
$$

$$
B=\{\text{tail pada second flip}\}=\{HT,TT\}.
$$

Then:

$$
P(A)=\frac12,
\qquad
P(B)=\frac12,
$$

and

$$
A\cap B=\{HT\},
$$

so

$$
P(A\cap B)=\frac14.
$$

Check:

$$
P(A)P(B)
=
\frac12\cdot\frac12
=
\frac14.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{A\text{ dan }B\text{ independent}.}
$$

Equivalent conditional check:

$$
P(B\mid A)
=
\frac{1/4}{1/2}
=
\frac12
=
P(B).
$$

---

# Section 5 — Independent vs Dependent: Two-Dice Examples

## 5.1 Independent Pair

Roll one red die and one white die.

Let:

$$
A=\{\text{red die}=4\},
$$

$$
B=\{\text{sum is odd}\}.
$$

There are 36 equally likely outcomes.

Hogg gives:

$$
P(A)=\frac6{36}=\frac16,
$$

$$
P(B)=\frac{18}{36}=\frac12,
$$

$$
P(A\cap B)=\frac3{36}=\frac1{12}.
$$

Check:

$$
P(A)P(B)
=
\frac16\cdot\frac12
=
\frac1{12}
=
P(A\cap B).
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{A\perp B}.
$$

---

## 5.2 Dependent Pair

Let:

$$
C=\{\text{red die}=5\},
$$

$$
D=\{\text{sum}=11\}.
$$

Hogg gives:

$$
P(C)=\frac6{36}=\frac16,
$$

$$
P(D)=\frac2{36}=\frac1{18},
$$

$$
P(C\cap D)=\frac1{36}.
$$

But

$$
P(C)P(D)
=
\frac16\cdot\frac1{18}
=
\frac1{108}.
$$

Since

$$
\frac1{36}\ne\frac1{108},
$$

we conclude:

$$
\boxed{C\text{ dan }D\text{ dependent}.}
$$

---

# Section 6 — Independence dan Complement

Hogg dan Miller both establish that if $A$ and $B$ are independent, then the following are also independent:

$$
\boxed{A\text{ and }B^c}
$$

$$
\boxed{A^c\text{ and }B}
$$

$$
\boxed{A^c\text{ and }B^c}
$$

---

## 6.1 Proof for $A$ and $B^c$

Start with:

$$
A=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c),
$$

where the two pieces are disjoint.

Therefore:

$$
P(A)
=
P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c).
$$

Since $A$ and $B$ are independent:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).
$$

So:

$$
P(A\cap B^c)
=
P(A)-P(A)P(B).
$$

Factor:

$$
P(A\cap B^c)
=
P(A)[1-P(B)].
$$

Because

$$
1-P(B)=P(B^c),
$$

we obtain:

$$
\boxed{
P(A\cap B^c)
=
P(A)P(B^c)
}.
$$

Thus $A$ and $B^c$ are independent.

---

## 6.2 Useful Exam Consequence

Jika $A$ dan $B$ independent:

$$
P(A\cap B^c)
=
P(A)[1-P(B)],
$$

$$
P(A^c\cap B)
=
[1-P(A)]P(B),
$$

$$
P(A^c\cap B^c)
=
[1-P(A)][1-P(B)].
$$

Hence:

$$
P(A\cup B)
=
1-P(A^c\cap B^c)
$$

$$
=
1-[1-P(A)][1-P(B)].
$$

Equivalent expansion:

$$
\boxed{
P(A\cup B)
=
P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B)
}
$$

when $A$ and $B$ are independent.

---

# Section 7 — Independence vs Mutually Exclusive

Ini salah satu **exam traps paling penting**.

## Mutually Exclusive

$$
A\cap B=\varnothing
$$

sehingga

$$
P(A\cap B)=0.
$$

## Independent

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).
$$

Jika $A$ dan $B$ mutually exclusive dengan:

$$
P(A)>0,
\qquad
P(B)>0,
$$

maka

$$
P(A)P(B)>0,
$$

tetapi

$$
P(A\cap B)=0.
$$

Jadi mereka **tidak independent**.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{mutually exclusive + both positive probability}
\Rightarrow
\text{dependent}
}
$$

### Why intuitively?

Jika $A$ terjadi dan events mutually exclusive, maka $B$ must not occur:

$$
P(B\mid A)=0,
$$

which differs from

$$
P(B)>0.
$$

So learning that $A$ happened changes the probability of $B$ drastically.

> [!BUG] Independence Trap
> **Mutually exclusive** means “cannot occur together.”  
> **Independent** means “knowledge of one does not change the probability of the other.”
>
> They are conceptually almost opposite when both events have positive probability.

---

# Section 8 — Special Case: Zero-Probability Event

Hogg points out a subtle consequence of the product definition.

Jika

$$
P(A)=0,
$$

then because

$$
A\cap B\subseteq A,
$$

we have

$$
P(A\cap B)=0.
$$

Also:

$$
P(A)P(B)=0.
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).
$$

So according to the formal definition:

$$
\boxed{
A \text{ is independent of every event }B
}
$$

when $P(A)=0$.

This is one reason product-form independence is more general than conditional-form independence.

---

# Section 9 — Pairwise Independence

Untuk tiga events $A,B,C$, **pairwise independence** means each pair is independent:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B),
$$

$$
P(A\cap C)=P(A)P(C),
$$

$$
P(B\cap C)=P(B)P(C).
$$

This checks only intersections of size 2.

It does **not** automatically guarantee:

$$
P(A\cap B\cap C)
=
P(A)P(B)P(C).
$$

---

# Section 10 — Pairwise Independence ≠ Mutual Independence

Hogg gives a classic example.

Urn contains four equally likely balls numbered:

$$
\{1,2,3,4\}.
$$

Define:

$$
A=\{1,2\},
$$

$$
B=\{1,3\},
$$

$$
C=\{1,4\}.
$$

Each event has probability:

$$
P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=\frac12.
$$

Pairwise intersections:

$$
A\cap B=\{1\},
$$

$$
A\cap C=\{1\},
$$

$$
B\cap C=\{1\}.
$$

Thus:

$$
P(A\cap B)
=
P(A\cap C)
=
P(B\cap C)
=
\frac14.
$$

And:

$$
P(A)P(B)
=
P(A)P(C)
=
P(B)P(C)
=
\frac14.
$$

So all pairs are independent.

However:

$$
A\cap B\cap C=\{1\},
$$

thus:

$$
P(A\cap B\cap C)=\frac14.
$$

But:

$$
P(A)P(B)P(C)
=
\frac12\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac12
=
\frac18.
$$

Since:

$$
\frac14\ne\frac18,
$$

the three events are **not mutually independent**.

> [!DANGER] Pairwise-vs-Mutual Trap
> Checking all pairwise products is insufficient when there are $3+$ events.

---

# Section 11 — Mutual Independence for Three Events

For events $A,B,C$ to be mutually independent, Miller requires:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B),
$$

$$
P(A\cap C)=P(A)P(C),
$$

$$
P(B\cap C)=P(B)P(C),
$$

and also

$$
\boxed{
P(A\cap B\cap C)
=
P(A)P(B)P(C).
}
$$

Thus there are **four conditions** for complete independence of three events.

### Shortcut memory

```text
3 events:
all pairs
+
triple
```

---

# Section 12 — Mutual Independence for More Than Three Events

For events

$$
A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_k,
$$

mutual independence requires the product rule for **every intersection of any 2, 3, ..., k events**.

For example, for any selected indices:

$$
i_1,\ldots,i_r,
\qquad 2\le r\le k,
$$

we require:

$$
\boxed{
P(A_{i_1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_r})
=
\prod_{j=1}^{r}P(A_{i_j})
}.
$$

So independence of many events is stronger than simply:

$$
P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_k)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{k}P(A_i).
$$

All lower-order intersections must also satisfy factorization.

---

# Section 13 — Product Rule for Independent Events

If $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ are mutually independent, then:

$$
\boxed{
P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_k)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{k}P(A_i)
}.
$$

This is especially useful in repeated independent trials.

### Example — Five Independent Coin Tosses

For a fair coin, suppose five tosses are independent.

Probability of exact sequence HHTHT:

$$
P(HHTHT)
=
\left(\frac12\right)^5
=
\frac1{32}.
$$

Every fixed five-toss sequence has probability:

$$
\frac1{32}.
$$

This links independence directly to the Binomial mechanism later in [[2.5 Distribusi Diskrit Umum]].

---

# Section 14 — Repeated Independent Trials vs Without Replacement

Independence often comes from a physical mechanism where each trial resets to the same probability structure.

## With Replacement

Urn contains 2 red and 4 white balls.

Draw one ball, replace it, then draw again.

Each draw has:

$$
P(R)=\frac26=\frac13,
$$

$$
P(W)=\frac46=\frac23.
$$

Because replacement restores composition, successive draw events can be modeled as independent.

For sequence $WWRWR$:

$$
P(WWRWR)
=
\left(\frac23\right)^3
\left(\frac13\right)^2.
$$

---

## Without Replacement

If no replacement occurs, composition changes after each draw.

Then generally:

$$
P(\text{second red}\mid\text{first red})
\ne
P(\text{second red}).
$$

So successive draw events are generally dependent.

> [!IMPORTANT] Replacement is a clue, not the definition
> “With replacement” often produces independence in standard urn models, while “without replacement” often produces dependence.  
> But formally, always use a probability criterion when asked to **prove/check** independence.

---

# Section 15 — Worked Example: Test Independence from Given Probabilities

Suppose:

$$
P(A)=0.4,
$$

$$
P(B)=0.7,
$$

$$
P(A\cap B)=0.28.
$$

Check:

$$
P(A)P(B)
=
0.4(0.7)
=
0.28.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{A\text{ dan }B\text{ independent}.}
$$

Then:

$$
P(A\mid B)
=
P(A)
=
0.4.
$$

and:

$$
P(B\mid A)
=
P(B)
=
0.7.
$$

Also:

$$
P(A^c\cap B)
=
(1-0.4)(0.7)
=
0.42.
$$

---

# Section 16 — Worked Example: Detect Dependence Quickly

Suppose:

$$
P(A)=0.3,
$$

$$
P(B)=0.5,
$$

$$
P(A\cap B)=0.10.
$$

Independent benchmark:

$$
P(A)P(B)
=
0.3(0.5)
=
0.15.
$$

Actual joint:

$$
0.10.
$$

Because:

$$
0.10\ne0.15,
$$

events are dependent.

Equivalent conditional comparison:

$$
P(B\mid A)
=
\frac{0.10}{0.30}
=
\frac13,
$$

while

$$
P(B)=\frac12.
$$

Knowledge of $A$ changes $B$'s probability.

---

# Section 17 — Integrated Probability Calculations

## 17.1 At Least One of Two Independent Events

If $A$ and $B$ independent:

$$
P(A^c\cap B^c)
=
P(A^c)P(B^c).
$$

Hence:

$$
P(A\cup B)
=
1-P(A^c\cap B^c)
$$

$$
=
1-[1-P(A)][1-P(B)].
$$

This is often the fastest method for “at least one”.

---

## 17.2 Exactly One of Two Independent Events

Exactly one occurs:

$$
(A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B).
$$

These two events are disjoint, so:

$$
P(\text{exactly one})
=
P(A)P(B^c)+P(A^c)P(B).
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
P(\text{exactly one})
=
P(A)[1-P(B)]
+
[1-P(A)]P(B)
}.
$$

---

## 17.3 Neither of Two Independent Events

$$
P(A^c\cap B^c)
=
\boxed{
[1-P(A)][1-P(B)]
}.
$$

---

# Section 18 — Independence Calculation Workflow

Gunakan workflow berikut.

## Step 1 — Define events

Write:

$$
A=\{\cdots\},
\qquad
B=\{\cdots\}.
$$

## Step 2 — Determine available quantities

Apakah soal memberi:

- $P(A),P(B),P(A\cap B)$?
- conditional probability?
- counts from equally likely outcomes?
- repeated-trial structure?

## Step 3 — Choose criterion

Most direct:

$$
P(A\cap B)\stackrel{?}=P(A)P(B).
$$

Or, if conditional is given:

$$
P(B\mid A)\stackrel{?}=P(B).
$$

## Step 4 — Calculate both sides

Do not declare independence from narrative intuition alone.

## Step 5 — Conclude explicitly

Either:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
\Rightarrow
\text{independent}
$$

or:

$$
P(A\cap B)\ne P(A)P(B)
\Rightarrow
\text{dependent}.
$$

## Step 6 — For $3+$ events

Check **all required intersections**, not only pairs.

# Section 19 — Exam Traps

## 19.1 Mutually Exclusive Trap

Wrong:

> “They cannot occur together, therefore they are independent.”

For positive-probability mutually exclusive events:

$$
P(A\cap B)=0
$$

but

$$
P(A)P(B)>0.
$$

Therefore dependent.

---

## 19.2 Pairwise Independence Trap

For three events, checking:

$$
A\perp B,
\quad
A\perp C,
\quad
B\perp C
$$

does **not** prove mutual independence.

Triple intersection must also factor.

---

## 19.3 Product-without-Justification Trap

Do not write:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
$$

unless independence is stated or established.

The universal multiplication rule is:

$$
P(A\cap B)
=
P(A)P(B\mid A).
$$

Only under independence does this simplify to:

$$
P(A)P(B).
$$

---

## 19.4 Conditional-Independence Direction Trap

If independence holds:

$$
P(B\mid A)=P(B)
$$

and

$$
P(A\mid B)=P(A),
$$

provided relevant denominators are positive.

Do not compare:

$$
P(B\mid A)
$$

with

$$
P(A).
$$

Wrong objects.

---

## 19.5 “Different Experiments” Assumption Trap

Two events arising from separate-looking parts of a story are not automatically independent.

Independence is a property of their joint probability structure.

---

## 19.6 Without-Replacement Trap

Without replacement usually changes future probabilities.

If:

$$
P(B\mid A)\ne P(B),
$$

then events are dependent.

---

# Section 20 — Formula Map

| Concept | Formula | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Two-event independence | $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$ | Definition |
| Conditional equivalent | $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$ | $P(A)>0$ |
| Reverse conditional equivalent | $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ | $P(B)>0$ |
| Complement independence | $A\perp B\Rightarrow A\perp B^c,\ A^c\perp B,\ A^c\perp B^c$ | Theorem |
| Independent union | $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B)$ | $A\perp B$ |
| Neither | $P(A^c\cap B^c)=[1-P(A)][1-P(B)]$ | $A\perp B$ |
| Pairwise independence | each pair intersection factors | $3+$ events |
| Mutual independence | every intersection of any subset factors | $3+$ events |
| Triple mutual condition | pairs factor + $P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A)P(B)P(C)$ | Three events |
| Independent sequence | $P(\cap_iA_i)=\prod_iP(A_i)$ | Mutually independent |

# Section 21 — Relationship Map

## 21.1 Connection to [[1.4 Probabilitas Bersyarat]]

Independence is exactly the case where conditioning produces no change:

$$
P(B\mid A)=P(B).
$$

This converts the multiplication rule:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)
$$

into:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).
$$

---

## 21.2 Connection to [[1.6 Teorema Bayes dan Hukum Probabilitas Total]]

Bayes problems frequently involve **dependent** events because observing evidence changes posterior probability.

If:

$$
P(A\mid B)=P(A),
$$

then $B$ carries no probability information about $A$.

---

## 21.3 Connection to [[2.5 Distribusi Diskrit Umum]]

Binomial distribution relies on repeated Bernoulli trials with independence assumptions.

The factor:

$$
p^x(1-p)^{n-x}
$$

comes from multiplication of probabilities across independent trials.

---

## 21.4 Connection to [[4.1 Penarikan Sampel Acak]]

Random samples from an infinite population are modeled as independent and identically distributed random variables.

Thus event independence here becomes part of the probabilistic foundation for later iid sampling.

---
